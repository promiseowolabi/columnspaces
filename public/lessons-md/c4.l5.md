# C4.L5 — SIMD, Morsels and the Limits

_Track C4: Vectorized Execution · ~18 min · columnspaces_

> A 512-bit register holds 8 int64 lanes, so a perfectly vectorised filter has a ceiling of 8× on instruction count — and none of it reduces the bytes that have to cross the memory system. Which is why this track is fourth and not first.
You have removed the per-row overhead (C4.L1), stopped copying survivors (C4.L2), stopped assembling tuples (C4.L3) and made the aggregate mergeable (C4.L4). Two mechanisms remain for making the work that is left go faster, and then there is a wall.

This lesson is the wall, and the two mechanisms are only interesting because of where they stop.

**SIMD** widens the instruction: one comparison against several values at once. **Morsel-driven parallelism** widens the machine: many cores on one query, dispatched in small units of work. Both are real, both are worth having, and both are multipliers on a term that C0 and C2 already decided the size of. The most valuable sentence in this track is the one that says so out loud — and it is a sentence you may have to say to someone who has just proposed spending a quarter on SIMD kernels.

---

## What SIMD actually buys, and what the compiler will do unasked

The arithmetic is lanes:

```
512-bit register ÷ 64-bit values  =   8 lanes
512-bit register ÷ 32-bit values  =  16 lanes
512-bit register ÷  8-bit values  =  64 lanes
```

That is the ceiling on the *instruction count* for a comparison-heavy loop, and it is why the width of your physical type is an execution decision and not only a storage one. C1's bit-packing and frame-of-reference narrowed values to shrink bytes; the same narrowing doubles the lanes. A predicate over `int32` dictionary codes gets 16 comparisons per instruction where the same predicate over `int64` timestamps gets 8. **Encoding and execution are the same decision seen twice** — that is C1.L5's third payoff, stated in lanes.

You do not usually write the intrinsics. What matters more is knowing what the compiler will vectorise on its own, because the batched loop shape from C4.L1 was chosen to qualify:

**It will, given a loop that is:**
- **countable** — the trip count is known before entry, so it can be split into a vector body and a scalar tail
- **branch-free in the body** — the comparison result is *written*, not jumped on. This is the reason the batched model takes **192 branches** at a vector width of 2,048 where the tuple loop takes **350,967**
- **unit-stride and contiguous** — consecutive addresses, one array per operand
- **non-aliasing** — the compiler must be able to prove the output does not overlap the input
- **call-free** — no function calls in the body, virtual or otherwise

**It will not, or not well:**
- **a data-dependent branch per row.** The volcano loop is unvectorisable in principle, not by accident: its control flow depends on each row's value, and there is no vector form of "return from this iterator".
- **a gather through a selection vector.** Hardware gather instructions exist and are far weaker than a contiguous load, which is exactly the case C4.L3 named as late materialization's loss condition. This is the honest cost of addressing rather than compacting: dense arrays vectorise, scattered positions do not.
- **variable-length data.** Strings are a length and a pointer; comparing two of them is a loop, not an instruction. Engines that vectorise string work do it by comparing *fixed-size prefixes* first — which is why a string vector usually carries an inlined prefix.
- **anything behind a virtual call**, which is the whole operator interface of a row-at-a-time engine.
- **floating-point reductions**, unless you have explicitly allowed reassociation — because summing in a different order gives a different answer, and the compiler will not silently change your results.

That last one is the same principle as `compressed_path` being graded as an equivalence: a fast path is only allowed if the answer is identical, and where it cannot be, somebody has to opt in with their eyes open.

## Morsels, and why static partitioning loses to skew

Vectorisation makes one core efficient. Getting sixteen cores onto one query is a scheduling problem, and the naive answer fails in a way you can compute.

**Static partitioning.** Split the input into as many pieces as there are threads, run, join. The query finishes when the *slowest* thread finishes — the makespan is a maximum, not a mean, which is the single most under-appreciated fact in parallel data processing. So with 16 threads and one partition holding 25% of the rows:

```
ideal share per thread   1/16  =  6.25% of the work
the skewed partition            25% of the work
makespan                        25% / 6.25%  =  4× the ideal
```

Fifteen cores are idle for three quarters of the time, and no amount of vectorised execution inside those cores fixes it. This is the same "the mean tells you nothing about a skewed job" observation C2.L3 made about clustering depth, arriving in the scheduler.

**Morsel-driven parallelism.** Cut the input into many small fixed-size units — morsels — and let each worker *pull* the next one when it finishes the last. The imbalance is then bounded by a single morsel rather than by a partition:

```
40,000,000 rows ÷ 131,072-row morsels  =  306 morsels
306 morsels ÷ 16 workers               ≈  19 per worker
worst-case tail                         ≈  1/19  ≈  5% over ideal
```

That is the whole argument: **work-stealing turns a skew problem into a granularity problem**, and a granularity problem has a knob with computable settings. Two more properties come with it and both matter in practice:

- **It is robust to heterogeneity you did not model.** A core throttling, a noisy neighbour, one morsel whose rows are all nulls — the puller absorbs all of it without the planner having predicted anything.
- **It gives you a place to put NUMA locality.** A worker preferring morsels whose data is on its own socket is a scheduling preference, not a plan rewrite, because the unit of work is small and interchangeable.

And the same crossover as C4.L1, one level up: morsels too small and dispatch overhead dominates (a queue operation per morsel, plus a shared table probe); morsels too large and the tail returns. There is no universally correct size, and the honest form of the answer is the arithmetic above with your row count in it.

The non-obvious consequence: **the granularity of parallelism is usually set by your physical layout, not by your scheduler.** If the unit a scan can hand out is a row group, then a table with fewer row groups than you have cores cannot use your cores — and row-group size was a C2 decision made months earlier by whoever wrote the loader.

_fig 1 — three factors, in the order the course teaches them, and the wall behind all of them_

Components: 40 GB table · one recurring query (40 columns · 365 days · the bill is bytes scanned) · projection: 4 columns of 40  ->  4.0 GB (factor 10, decided by the query text and the physical layout (C0.L1)) · pruning: 7 days of 365 at depth 1  ->  77 MB (factor 52, decided by write order and statistics — and it silently stops working (C2.L3)) · execution: batches, selection vectors, encoded fast paths (10.39 -> 4.93 modelled operations per row, on the 77 MB that is left (C4.L1)) · SIMD: 8 lanes at 64 bits (16 at 32 bits — a ceiling on instructions, none on bytes) · morsels: tail bounded by one morsel (~5% over ideal at 19 morsels per worker; static partitioning was 4× under skew) · the 77 MB still crosses the memory system once (about 1 comparison per 8 bytes loaded — memory-bound, so instructions issued is not the binding constraint)

Steps:
1. One recurring query against a 40 GB table. Everything this course teaches is a factor applied to that number, and the factors multiply — which is why the order they are applied in decides where the engineering effort is worth spending.
2. Projection is first and largest: touching 4 column chunks of 40 is a factor of 10, available immediately, requiring nothing but that the query name its columns and the format store them separately.
3. Pruning is second: a seven-day window out of a year, on a layout whose clustering depth is 1, is another factor of 52. It is the most fragile factor in the course — an upstream loader dropping its ORDER BY takes it away with no error anywhere.
4. Only now does the execution model apply, and it applies to the 77 MB that survived: roughly halving the modelled operations per row, and much more than that where an encoded fast path lines up with the query.
5. SIMD and morsels sit inside that third factor. Lanes bound the instruction count at 8 for 64-bit values and 16 for 32-bit; morsel dispatch bounds the parallel tail at one morsel instead of one skewed partition. Both are multipliers on the remainder.
6. And behind all of it: those 77 MB have to cross the memory system, once, whatever the instruction count. A filter does about one comparison per eight bytes loaded, which puts it firmly in the memory-bound regime — so the last factor is bounded by bytes, exactly like the first two.

## The honest ceiling: it is bytes all the way down

Here is the arithmetic that closes the track. Take the filter you have been building: load a value, compare it against two bounds, write a position for the survivors. Per 8-byte `int64`:

```
bytes loaded per row     8
comparisons per row      1–2
arithmetic intensity     ≈ 0.125–0.25 operations per byte
```

Any modern core can issue several comparisons per cycle and cannot load anything like 8 bytes per cycle from DRAM. A kernel at a quarter of an operation per byte is therefore **memory-bound**, not compute-bound, and the consequence is uncomfortable if you have just spent a month on instruction counts: **removing instructions from a memory-bound loop does not remove the bound.** It removes headroom you were not using.

What *does* move a memory-bound scan is anything that reduces bytes touched or improves locality:

- **fewer columns** — projection, the C0 factor
- **fewer blocks** — pruning, the C2 factor
- **smaller values** — encoding, the C1 factor, which is also the lane-count factor
- **not decoding at all** — execute-on-compressed, where a run of 300,000 rows is summed from its header and the bytes are never streamed
- **cache residency** — which is what vector sizing is for, and the one real win in C4 that a count of operations cannot see

Read that list and notice what it is: **the first three tracks of this course, in order.** C4's mechanisms make the remaining work efficient, and "the remaining work" is a quantity C0, C1 and C2 already fixed.

**So state the ordering plainly, because it is the professional judgement this track exists to produce: the biggest win is usually still the first payoff — bytes — and that is why the course teaches them in that order.** If a query reads 40 GB with no pruning, a perfect execution engine is worth a fraction of one `ORDER BY` in the loader. If a query has already been cut to 77 MB by projection and pruning, then execution is what is left and C4 is where the remaining money is. Both sentences are true; the skill is knowing which one you are in, and the way you know is by computing the factors before you choose the work.

> **[warning]** Every number in C4 that came from the `batch-machine` lab is a **count of modelled operations**, and the lab says so on its own last panel. That choice is deliberate — a count is identical on every machine, which is what makes these lessons replayable and the labs gradeable — but it cuts both ways, and the caveat belongs in your own presentation of these numbers:
> 
> - **It undercounts the batched machine.** One comparison per row is charged identically whether the loop is scalar or eight-wide, so the model cannot see the lane win at all.
> - **It is silent about locality.** Two loops with the same operation count and different working-set sizes are the same number here and are not the same program.
> - **It cannot show the gather penalty.** A position into a resident array and a position into a compressed page are the same lookup in the model and are not remotely the same operation in an engine (C4.L3).
> - **It is not a clock, and cannot be turned into one.** "2.1× fewer operations" is not "2.1× faster" in either direction.
> 
> What the real thing adds, named rather than gestured at: SIMD lane width, cache and TLB residency, instruction-level parallelism, hardware prefetch behaviour, and the cost of decompressing a page you did not need. If you want those, you need a profiler and a machine, and the honest metrics are still counts — **instructions retired per output row** and **bytes moved per output row** — because those two survive a change of hardware and a wall-clock number does not.

> **[vendor snapshot — verified 2026-09]** Where parallel granularity is actually set, and a vendor CPU claim that is exactly a C4 claim
>
> **The granularity fact, and why it belongs to C2 rather than to C4.** DuckDB's performance guide states that it "parallelizes the workload based on *row groups*", that the default row group size in its database format is **122,880 rows**, and — the sentence to write down — that "for a query to run on *k* threads, it needs to scan at least *k* × 122,880 rows". The size is settable per database (`ROW_GROUP_SIZE` on `ATTACH`), and the same page notes that launching too many threads, for instance because of HyperThreading, can itself cause slowdowns.
> 
> That is the morsel arithmetic in this lesson as an operational constraint: **a small table cannot use your cores, and the unit of parallelism was chosen by whoever sized the row groups.** It is also a rare case where a storage parameter and a scheduling parameter are the same number, which is why C2's row-group sizing lesson and this one are about one decision.
> 
> **The vendor claim, treated as a claim.** VAST's own feature page states that VAST DataBase "consistently outperforms legacy and cloud object-based systems, delivering up to 20× faster query performance on highly selective workloads while using up to 90% less CPU". **That is VAST's claim, on VAST's configuration, against an unnamed comparison basis; no cluster was run for this course and nothing here measures it.** The same page describes the durable architecture behind it: writes land in Storage Class Memory and are converted in the background into **32 KB columnar chunks** on flash, and under DASE "all compute nodes (CNodes) can directly access every data node (DNode)", so added compute contributes query capacity "no rebalancing, coordination, or data redistribution required".
> 
> Now do the thing this course exists to teach. "90% less CPU" is not a storage claim — it is precisely a **C4 claim**, about how much work per row an execution model issues, and "on highly selective workloads" is precisely a **C0/C2 claim**, about how few bytes a selective predicate has to touch. So the two are entangled in exactly the way this lesson warns about, and a proof-of-concept that reports a single ratio has measured nothing you can design against.
> 
> What a falsifiable evaluation would have to hold fixed and report as counts:
> 
> - **bytes scanned per query**, at matched projection and matched selectivity — otherwise you are comparing pruning, not execution
> - **instructions retired per output row** (or CPU-seconds per byte scanned, since that is what "less CPU" must reduce to) — otherwise "less CPU" is a claim about the workload rather than the engine
> - **clustering depth on the columns your predicates name**, before and after your real load pattern — because a selective workload on a badly clustered table is not selective in bytes
> - **the same measurements at a cardinality that does not fit the group table** (C4.L4), since that is where every vectorised engine's advantage narrows
> 
> Any platform, ours or theirs, deserves that test rather than that sentence. The vendor room grades exactly this shape of plan, and `poc_undefined` is a severity-3 objection for a reason.

- **8 / 16** — SIMD lanes at 64-bit and 32-bit width (A 512-bit register divided by the value width. Narrower physical types double the lanes, which is why C1's encoding choices are also execution choices.)
- **192 vs 350,967** — branches, batched versus tuple-at-a-time (Modelled counts at a vector width of 2,048 over 131,072 rows. A branch-free countable loop is the precondition for any auto-vectorisation at all.)
- **4× vs ~5%** — makespan penalty under skew: static partitioning versus morsels (16 threads with one partition holding 25% of the work finishes 4× later than ideal. Morsels of 131,072 rows over 40M rows give 19 per worker, bounding the tail at roughly one morsel.)
- **0.125** — operations per byte for an int64 filter (One comparison per eight bytes loaded. Far into the memory-bound regime, which is why removing instructions from this loop removes headroom rather than the bound.)

> **[info]** Someone will propose the execution work before the layout work, because execution work is more interesting and reads better in a design document. The reply is arithmetic, and it takes three numbers:
> 
> 1. **Bytes the query reads today.** From the query profile or the footer arithmetic in C0.L1. Say it as a count.
> 2. **Bytes it would read with correct projection and clustering.** The factor is usually 10× to 100× and it is computable from metadata alone (C2.L3), without running anything.
> 3. **Operations per row the current engine issues.** Two if you have a profiler, or the modelled range from C4.L1 if you do not: roughly 10 per row row-at-a-time, roughly 5 batched.
> 
> Then: *"Execution is a factor of about two on the bytes that survive projection and pruning. Projection and pruning are a factor of five hundred on the bytes. We should do them in that order, and here is what each is worth in bytes scanned per day."*
> 
> That is not an argument against C4. It is C4 correctly sized — and the reason the same reviewer will listen when you later say that the query is already down to 77 MB, the engine is now the bottleneck, and the next win is in the execution model.

_the ceiling ≡ three limits you already respect_

- **Amdahl's law** (Speeding up the part you optimised bounds you by the part you did not. The honest first step is measuring the fraction, not choosing the optimisation.) ≡ **the three factors** (Execution acts on the bytes projection and pruning left. Optimising it perfectly still leaves those two factors in front of it, which is why they are taught first.)
- **the roofline model** (A kernel's operations-per-byte places it left or right of the machine's ridge point, and to the left the only thing that helps is touching fewer bytes or reusing them better.) ≡ **a columnar filter at 0.125 ops/byte** (Firmly to the left. So the wins are fewer columns, fewer blocks, narrower values, no decode, and cache-sized vectors — not more instructions per cycle.)
- **dynamic scheduling instead of a static split** (You stopped hand-sharding a parallel loop the day one shard took four times as long. A work queue turns an unpredictable distribution into a granularity choice.) ≡ **morsel-driven parallelism** (The same fix at query scale, with the same knob: too fine and dispatch dominates, too coarse and the tail returns — and the unit is usually inherited from the storage layout.)

**Q1. A team proposes a quarter of engineering time writing hand-vectorised filter kernels. The query in question scans 38 GB per run: it projects 34 of 40 columns and its clustering depth on the filtered column is 61 out of 64 row groups. What do you recommend, with arithmetic?**
   A. Approve it — filter kernels are on the hot path, and a 4–8× improvement in the inner loop is the largest single change available
   B. Fix bytes first: projection down to the 4 columns the query needs is roughly a factor of 8, and a rewrite that restores clustering depth toward 1 is roughly another factor of 20, so the same quarter spent on layout is worth two orders of magnitude against a lane-bounded factor on the work that is left
   C. Approve it, but only after buying more memory, since a memory-bound kernel needs bandwidth headroom before SIMD helps
   D. Reject it and scale the cluster instead: more cores multiply the existing kernels without any engineering risk
   Answer: B — Both factors are computable before anyone writes code, and they are multiplicative. Projecting 4 columns instead of 34 is about 8×; depth 61 means the query reads roughly 61 of 64 row groups where a well-clustered layout would read about 1, so the pruning factor available is around 20× even after allowing for a wide window. Against that, SIMD is bounded by lanes — 8 at 64-bit — on the bytes that survive, and the loop is memory-bound at roughly 0.125 operations per byte, so removing instructions removes headroom rather than the bound. Scaling the cluster makes the symptom disappear while the bill rises, which is the cost-is-a-count trap from C0. The kernels may well be worth doing later, once the query reads tens of megabytes and execution is genuinely what is left.

**Q2. Your engine partitions each scan statically across 16 worker threads. A tenant's data is heavily skewed, and one partition holds about 25% of the matching rows. What is the effect, and what fixes it?**
   A. Throughput drops about 25%, and the fix is to increase the thread count so each partition is smaller
   B. The query takes about 4× the ideal time because the makespan is the slowest thread, not the average — and the fix is dispatching many small morsels that workers pull on completion, which bounds the imbalance at one morsel instead of one partition
   C. Nothing measurable: the scheduler rebalances automatically once a thread finishes early
   D. Results become non-deterministic, since partitions finish out of order and the aggregate merges in arrival order
   Answer: B — The makespan of a parallel phase is the maximum over its workers, so a partition holding 25% of the work against an ideal share of 6.25% means the whole query waits 4× longer than it needed to while 15 cores idle. Raising the thread count does not help while the split is static — it makes the ideal share smaller and leaves the skewed partition unchanged. Morsel-driven dispatch converts the problem into a granularity choice: with 40M rows in 131,072-row morsels there are about 306 morsels and 19 per worker, so the tail is roughly 5% over ideal, and the same mechanism absorbs throttled cores and noisy neighbours you never modelled. Determinism is a separate concern; a mergeable aggregate gives the same answer regardless of arrival order, which is exactly why C4.L4 insists on mergeability.

**Q3. Which loop will a compiler auto-vectorise, and why does the answer explain the shape of the batched filter in forge lab 03?**
   A. A row-at-a-time loop that calls next() on an operator and returns early on non-matching rows, because the branch is predictable
   B. A countable loop over a contiguous array that writes the comparison result instead of branching on it, with no calls and no aliasing between input and output — which is precisely why the batched filter takes 192 branches over 131,072 rows instead of 350,967
   C. Either one — modern compilers vectorise across function calls and data-dependent branches
   D. Neither: SQL predicates are too dynamic to vectorise, which is why engines ship hand-written intrinsics only
   Answer: B — Auto-vectorisation needs a known trip count, straight-line code in the body, unit-stride access and provable non-aliasing. A per-row loop that exits an iterator fails all four, which is why the volcano model is unvectorisable in principle rather than by accident. The batched filter was written to qualify: one pass over a dense array, the predicate result written rather than jumped on, no calls inside the body. That is also the honest limit of the counting model — it charges one comparison per row either way and so cannot see the lane win at all — and it is why a gather through a selection vector remains the case that does not vectorise well, which C4.L3 named as late materialization's loss condition.

**Leis, Boncz, Kemper and Neumann, "Morsel-Driven Parallelism: A NUMA-Aware Query Evaluation Framework for the Many-Core Age" (SIGMOD 2014)** is the source for the second half of this lesson. Read it for three things the summary version loses: that the morsel size is a tuning parameter with a measured sweet spot, that the dispatcher's NUMA preference is what makes the design worth the complexity on a multi-socket machine, and that elasticity — changing the degree of parallelism mid-query — falls out of pull-based dispatch for free.

**Polychroniou, Raghavan and Ross, "Rethinking SIMD Vectorization for In-Memory Databases" (SIGMOD 2015)** is the source for the first half, and it is the corrective to most SIMD folklore: it works through selection scans, hash tables, partitioning and sorting, and is explicit about where the vector width does *not* translate into a proportional win because the operation is a gather or a scatter. **Lemire and Boytsov, "Decoding billions of integers per second through vectorization"** is the companion for the decode side, and the honest way to size how much the decode you avoided was actually costing.

For the ceiling itself, **Williams, Waterman and Patterson, "Roofline: An Insightful Visual Performance Model for Multicore Architectures" (2009)**. It is not a database paper and it is the most useful thing on this page: once you can place a kernel's operations-per-byte against a machine's ridge point, arguments about instruction counts in memory-bound loops end quickly.

And **Kersten et al. (VLDB 2018)** once more, because it is the only paper that gives both execution models a fair test on the same hardware, and its conclusion — that the choice is workload-dependent and the differences are smaller than either camp claims — is the correct attitude to carry out of this track.

**Where C4 lands.** You have built a batched engine, proved its fast paths are equivalences rather than optimisations, and sized what execution is worth against what layout is worth. **C5** takes the thing every column store is bad at: change. Write buffers, delta stores, merge-on-read against copy-on-write, and the amplification arithmetic that makes one row update rewrite a row group — which is also where VAST's architecture stops being a footnote and becomes the subject.
