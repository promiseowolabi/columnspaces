# A1.L4 — Multi-Tenancy in a Warehouse

_Track A1: Solution Architecture · ~18 min · columnspaces_

> One of your 400 tenants holds 10% of the bytes and is 40× the mean. The mean tenant does not exist, which makes every per-tenant average in your design document a fiction — including the one your chargeback model is built on.
**Four hundred tenants, 180 TiB, and no average tenant.** Here is `tenancy-desk`'s worked population, drawn Zipf from the shared seeded generator so every reader sees the same tenants:

```text
mean tenant       460.80 GiB   ← the number in most design documents
median tenant     163.77 GiB   ← the tenant you will never have trouble with
largest tenant     17.99 TiB   ← 10.0% of all bytes, 39.98× the mean, 112× the median
smallest tenant    81.88 GiB
top 1% of tenants  27.7% of all bytes
```

Every architectural decision below follows from the shape of that list rather than from its length. **The largest tenant sets your capacity ceiling and your noisy-neighbour blast radius. The smallest sets your per-query metadata overhead**, because a tiny tenant still opens whole file footers to plan a query and gets no discount for having little data. A model built on 460.80 GiB is wrong at both ends simultaneously, and it is wrong in the direction that looks fine in a spreadsheet.

So the deliverable is four numbers and a boundary: the chosen model, the isolation boundary it can actually enforce, the attribution method, and the worst-tenant figure. `tenancy-desk` grades five checks over them — `isolation`, `attribution`, `worst_tenant`, `catalog_ceiling` and `noisy_neighbour` — and two of the five fail for a *claim* rather than an arithmetic error.

---

## The four models, and the five the desk actually distinguishes

The families everyone names are table-per-tenant, schema-per-tenant, shared-table-with-a-filter and cluster-per-tenant. The desk splits the third one, and the split is the whole isolation story:

| model | strongest boundary it can enforce | catalog objects here | attribution that works |
|---|---|---|---|
| `shared-table-clustered` (tenant id is a *sort key*) | **row-filter** | 756,003 | query tags, query-log bytes, per-tenant pool |
| `shared-table-partitioned` (tenant id is a *partition*) | **partition** | 756,003 | all of the above **plus** storage bytes by partition |
| `table-per-tenant` | **table** | 757,200 | all of the above |
| `schema-per-tenant` | **schema** | 757,200 | all of the above |
| `account-per-tenant` (cluster-per-tenant) | **account** | 757,200 | account-level billing |

Read the first two rows carefully, because they are the same physical layout in most people's heads and they are not the same design. **In a clustered shared table the tenant column is a sort key, not a boundary: it changes which blocks are read, never which blocks may be read.** The only enforcement point left is a row filter applied by the query planner — which means a planner bug or a forgotten predicate is a data breach rather than a wrong number. `isolation` fails a submission that claims a partition boundary under a clustered model, and the desk's reasoning is worth quoting: *a boundary that does not exist is worse than a weak one, because it stops anyone looking for the compensating control.*

Now the part that contradicts folklore. **The catalog difference between shared-table and table-per-tenant is 1,197 objects out of 756,003.** Look at where the objects come from:

```text
shared-table-partitioned:      3 tables + 108,000 partitions + 648,000 files = 756,003
table-per-tenant:          1,200 tables + 108,000 partitions + 648,000 files = 757,200
```

The **file term dominates**, and the file count is a property of the data and the commit interval rather than of the tenancy model. So "table-per-tenant will blow up the catalog" is, at these parameters, false — and the true version is more useful: the catalog ceiling is reached by **adding tenants or shortening commit intervals**, not by choosing a model. Take the same design to 4,000 tenants and you get 1,080,000 partitions and 6,480,000 files — **7,560,003 objects against a ceiling of 1,000,000**, breached 7.5× over, with no tenant having grown at all.

What table-per-tenant does cost is the term the object count does not show: 1,200 tables that DDL, permissions, schema migrations and query planning must each enumerate. That is an **engineer-months** cost and a blast-radius cost, not a catalog-object cost, and saying so precisely is what separates a design review from a repetition of received wisdom.

## The tail: why the smallest tenant is a metadata problem

A query for one tenant in a shared table with 648,000 files opens the footers of its own slice:

```text
files per tenant query  =  648,000 ÷ 400            =  1,620
metadata per query      =  1,620 × 24 KiB footer    =  38.0 MiB
```

For the median tenant that is nothing. For a genuinely small tenant — the desk exports 4 MiB as the tail reference — it is **9.5× metadata amplification**: the tenant costs more to *plan* than to *read*, and there is no layout change that fixes it because the cost is proportional to the file list rather than to the data. Under `shared-table-clustered` it doubles to 75.9 MiB, because the tenant column cannot restrict the planner to its own partitions and footers get opened across the shared set — **19× amplification on a 4 MiB tenant.**

That is the number that makes a free tier expensive and the reason a long tail of tiny tenants can cost more in aggregate planning than the head costs in aggregate scanning. It also gives you the one design response that works: consolidate the tail's files, do not consolidate the tail's tenants.

## Attribution, which fails as a claim rather than a number

`attribution` is a discipline check and it is the most commercially consequential one in the desk, because of a single implication: **an unattributed shared pool means every tenant's cost is the mean, and the mean is a fiction.**

Submit a perfectly correct worst-tenant figure of 17.99 TiB with no attribution method and you still fail, and the desk's grounds are exactly right — *the omission is the problem, not the estimate; your figure can be exact and still undefendable, because you cannot show a tenant the bytes that were theirs.* Three things become impossible at once:

- **Chargeback.** You cannot bill or internally allocate against a mean when the head is 39.98× it. Either the head is subsidised by 399 other tenants or everyone is overcharged; both are discovered by a customer, not by you.
- **Renegotiation.** The commercial conversation with the head tenant requires the head tenant's number. Without it, the platform absorbs the growth of the one account that is growing fastest.
- **Noisy-neighbour diagnosis.** An unattributed pool reports one aggregate that never names the tenant consuming it, so the incident is "the platform is slow" for as long as it takes someone to write the query you should have built in.

And the method has to match the model. `storage-bytes-by-partition` is the natural choice and it **does not work under a clustered shared table**: there is no per-tenant partition to sum, tenant bytes are interleaved inside shared files, and the smallest unit you can attribute is a file holding many tenants. `account-level-billing` only attributes when each tenant has its own account. Query tags, per-tenant compute pools and bytes-scanned-from-the-query-log work everywhere — which is a strong argument for tagging every query with a tenant id on day one, before you need it.

## Noisy neighbours: a cap is a count of slots

`noisy_neighbour` passes when a per-tenant concurrency cap exists **and** it leaves the pool usable by everyone else. Both halves matter: a cap equal to the pool is not a cap.

The model estimates the head tenant's uncapped claim from its byte share — 10.0% of 180 TiB, so about **6 of 64 slots** on steady demand — and that figure is a floor rather than a forecast. It models proportional appetite, and the incident you actually get is a backfill: one tenant reprocessing two years of history is not consuming its byte share, it is consuming everything available. So the cap is not sized from the estimate. It is sized so that **the head tenant's worst hour still leaves the tail able to plan**: a cap of 16 leaves 48 slots for the other 399 tenants, and that sentence — a count, for a named population — is what the check is looking for.

Note which end of the distribution the control protects. The head can survive queueing; it has 17.99 TiB and a batch window. The tail cannot, because a 4 MiB tenant's query is 38.0 MiB of footer reads and a two-minute queue makes your product look broken to the customer least able to tolerate it.

---

- **39.98×** — largest tenant ÷ mean tenant (17.99 TiB against a 460.80 GiB mean across 400 tenants and 180 TiB. Also 112× the median. Capacity is set by the head, so a mean-sized plan is short by that factor.)
- **27.7%** — of all bytes held by the top 1% of tenants (Four tenants out of four hundred. This is what Zipf means operationally: the population has no typical member and both ends need their own line in the model.)
- **9.5×** — metadata amplification on a 4 MiB tenant (1,620 footers at 24 KiB is 38.0 MiB opened to plan a query against 4 MiB of data — 19× if the tenant column is only a sort key. The tail costs more to plan than to read.)
- **1,197** — extra catalog objects for table-per-tenant here (Out of 756,003. The file term dominates, so the real catalog risk is adding tenants or shortening commit intervals — 4,000 tenants breaches a 1,000,000 ceiling 7.5× over.)

_fig 1 — one population, two ends, five graded checks_

Components: shared + sort key (boundary: row-filter only) · shared + partition (boundary: partition) · table / schema (boundary: table, schema) · account / cluster (boundary: account, N× the ops) · isolation (claimed ≤ enforceable) · attribution (or every cost is the mean) · metadata cost (1,620 footers per query) · noisy neighbour (a cap is a count of slots) · the population is Zipf, so the mean is a fiction (mean 460.80 GiB · median 163.77 GiB · head 17.99 TiB · top 1% hold 27.7%) · the head sets capacity (10.0% of bytes → the ceiling and the blast radius) · the tail sets metadata (38.0 MiB of footers against 4 MiB of data = 9.5×) · the tenancy decision, with both ends itemised (model · boundary · attribution method · worst-tenant bytes) · cap: 16 of 64 slots (leaves 48 for the other 399 tenants) · tag every query on day one (the only attribution that works under every model)

Steps:
1. Five models, not four, and the split that matters is inside the shared table: a tenant column used as a sort key changes which blocks are read, while a tenant column used as a partition changes which blocks may be read.
2. Price each candidate on the same four axes rather than on a preference: the boundary it can actually enforce, whether per-tenant cost can be attributed at all, what one query costs in metadata, and what stops one tenant taking the pool.
3. Then the fact that governs every one of those axes: tenant sizes are Zipf-distributed, so the mean describes nobody and the median describes the tenant you will never have trouble with.
4. Which forces both ends into the model separately. The head holds a tenth of all bytes, so it sets the capacity ceiling and the blast radius of any shared resource you give it uncapped access to.
5. And the tail sets a cost the head never reveals: a tiny tenant still opens 1,620 file footers to plan a query, so its metadata bill is several times its data, and no layout change reduces a cost proportional to the file list.
6. The decision is then four stated things rather than a diagram: the model, the boundary it can enforce, how a tenant is charged, and the largest tenant in bytes — never the mean and never the median.
7. Two controls make it operable, and both are counts. A per-tenant slot cap sized so the head tenant's worst hour leaves the tail able to plan, and a tenant tag on every query written before anybody needs it.

> **[warning]** **"Tenants are isolated by the tenant_id filter in every query."** That is a row filter enforced by convention, and convention is not an access control. `isolation` fails a boundary claimed above what the storage layout can enforce, and the reason is not pedantic: a design that claims a boundary it does not have stops anyone from adding the compensating control — the view, the row-level policy, the mandatory predicate in the semantic layer — that would have made the claim true.
> 
> **"The average tenant is about 460 GiB, so 400 tenants at 460 GiB is our capacity plan."** Both halves of that are wrong in the same direction. The mean is arithmetically fine as a total and useless as a per-tenant figure, and `worst_tenant` specifically detects a submitted figure that is the mean or the median and says so: *a Zipf population has no typical member, so a mean-sized plan is short by 39.98×* on the tenant that will actually break something. Capacity is set by the head.
> 
> **"Costs are shared across the platform, so per-tenant cost is not meaningful."** This is the sentence that gets repeated for two years and then becomes a pricing crisis. It is also self-fulfilling: the reason per-tenant cost is not meaningful is that nobody tagged the queries, and the fix costs almost nothing on day one and a migration on day 700. `attribution` fails it outright, and the tell is that the same platform cannot answer "which tenant caused Tuesday" either.

> **[vendor snapshot — verified 2026-09]** A substrate where the metadata term is a different purchase — and what a POC would have to measure
>
> Two of the four axes above — metadata cost and the catalog ceiling — are consequences of one architectural assumption: that the catalog is a separate service enumerating objects on object storage. So the vendor claim worth taking seriously here is the one that changes the assumption rather than the count.
> 
> **What VAST states.** Its architecture is described as **DASE — Disaggregated, Shared-Everything**: storage media decoupled from the CPUs that manage them, and that storage shared across every server in the cluster *including all system metadata*, with the position that all data and metadata are globally accessible across the system. On the database side, the feature page states that no partitioning or sharding is required because every compute node accesses the whole dataset directly, and that added compute contributes query capacity with no rebalancing, coordination or data redistribution required.
> 
> **The mechanism, and which of this lesson's numbers it touches.** Be precise about scope, because this is where evaluations go wrong. If partitioning is not required, then the **1,620-footers-per-tenant-query arithmetic is not the arithmetic** — that figure is the product of a file list on object storage and a footer per file, and both terms are properties of the substrate rather than of tenancy. Metadata living in the same namespace as data also moves the catalog ceiling somewhere else. What it does **not** touch is the isolation column: an access boundary is a control-plane property, and shared-everything storage is an argument about where metadata operations are served, not about who may read a row. Neither does it touch attribution, which is a telemetry decision you make regardless of substrate.
> 
> **And the honest reading.** "No partitioning required" is a claim about a *design constraint disappearing*, not about metadata work being free. Something still enumerates, something still holds per-object state, and the question is whether that work is gone or moved inside the platform and off your runbook. The difference is commercial rather than technical: work inside the platform is capacity you buy, work in your runbook is engineer-time you staff. Nothing here is measured by this course and no figure on those pages is ours.
> 
> **What a proof-of-concept would have to measure** — because `poc_undefined` is a severity-3 objection in `the-vendor` room and "their documentation says partitioning is unnecessary" is not an evaluation:
> 
> 1. **Metadata operations at your tenant cardinality, not at a demo's.** 400 tenants and their year-three object count, with concurrent writers. If shared metadata is the differentiator, this is the measurement where it shows.
> 2. **The cost of one query for your smallest tenant against your largest.** That ratio is this lesson's 9.5× restated as a measurement, and it is the single most diagnostic number for a long tail.
> 3. **Whether a per-tenant boundary exists at storage level or only at query level**, and what enforces it. Ask for the mechanism, not the assurance.
> 4. **What one tenant's worst hour does to the other 399** — run the backfill during the tail's peak and record the tail's queue, in slots and in queries, not in adjectives.
> 5. **Which metric would reveal background metadata work falling behind**, and whether you can see it. If the answer is "there is none", ask what would move first if there were.

_tenancy models ≡ isolation you already reason about_

- **threads in one address space** (Cheapest possible sharing, and a pointer bug is not a wrong answer — it is another thread's data. Safety is a property of every line of code rather than of the runtime.) ≡ **shared table, tenant id as a sort key** (The same bargain: cheapest metadata, and the boundary is a predicate the planner must remember. A forgotten filter is a breach, which is why the desk caps this model at row-filter isolation.)
- **separate processes with OS-enforced memory boundaries** (The boundary is real and enforced by something other than your discipline. You pay in per-process overhead and in the plumbing needed to share anything deliberately.) ≡ **table-per-tenant or schema-per-tenant** (A real boundary at table or schema level, paid for in 1,200 objects that DDL, permissions and planning each enumerate — engineer-months and blast radius, not the 1,197 catalog objects folklore focuses on.)
- **cgroups and CPU quotas** (Isolation of the address space says nothing about who gets the CPU. The noisy neighbour is a scheduling problem and the answer is a quota expressed as a share, enforced by the kernel.) ≡ **the per-tenant slot cap** (16 of 64 slots, leaving 48 for the other 399 tenants. Identical shape and the same lesson: a cap equal to the pool is not a cap, and the population it protects is the tail rather than the head.)

**Q1. Your platform holds 400 tenants in one shared table clustered on tenant_id. Finance asks for per-tenant cost so they can move to usage-based internal charging. What do you tell them, and what does it cost to fix?**
   A. Divide platform cost by tenant count — with 400 tenants the per-tenant figure is a reasonable allocation basis
   B. Per-tenant cost is not currently attributable and dividing by tenant count would charge the mean, which the head tenant beats by 39.98×; the fix is tenant tags on every query and bytes-scanned-per-tenant from the query log, because storage bytes cannot be attributed when tenant rows are interleaved inside shared files
   C. Attribute by storage bytes per partition, which is the standard method and requires no application changes
   D. Defer the request until the platform moves to table-per-tenant, at which point attribution becomes straightforward
   Answer: B — Two facts have to be delivered together. The distribution makes the mean an indefensible charging basis — the head tenant would be subsidised by hundreds of others and someone eventually notices — and the chosen model makes the natural attribution method unavailable, because a sort key does not create a per-tenant partition to sum and the smallest attributable unit is a file holding many tenants. Query tags and query-log bytes work under every model, which is why they are the thing to build before anybody asks. Option three names a method the desk explicitly rejects for this model. Option four trades a week of telemetry work for a migration, and it leaves the interim period with no answer at all — which is how "costs are shared, so per-tenant cost is not meaningful" survives for two years and becomes a pricing crisis.

**Q2. A design review claims table-per-tenant is unaffordable because 400 tenants would overwhelm the catalog. You have the object counts: 757,200 for table-per-tenant against 756,003 for the shared table, with a ceiling of 1,000,000. What is the correct intervention?**
   A. Agree — 757,200 against a 1,000,000 ceiling leaves too little headroom for a growing platform
   B. Correct the argument and redirect it: the file term dominates both totals, so the model choice costs 1,197 objects while adding tenants or shortening commit intervals is what breaches the ceiling — 4,000 tenants on this same design needs 7,560,003 objects; the real cost of table-per-tenant is 1,200 tables that DDL, permissions and planning each enumerate
   C. Agree with the conclusion but for the metadata reason instead: table-per-tenant multiplies the footers opened per query
   D. Recommend the clustered shared table, which minimises catalog objects and therefore maximises headroom
   Answer: B — The arithmetic settles it: partitions and files are properties of the data volume and commit cadence, not of the tenancy model, so both designs carry the same 756,000 objects and the model choice moves 0.16% of the total. Accepting the conclusion on the wrong evidence is worse than it looks, because it leaves the team believing the catalog is safe when the actual growth path — more tenants, tighter commits — breaches the ceiling 7.5× over without any model change. Option three is factually wrong in this model: footers per tenant query come from the file list, which is unchanged. Option four optimises the term that barely moves while giving up the isolation boundary, which is the worst trade available on this page. The useful correction names the true cost of table-per-tenant in engineer-months and blast radius rather than in objects.

**Q3. You are sizing a per-tenant concurrency cap on a 64-slot pool. The largest tenant holds 10% of the bytes, which the model puts at roughly 6 slots of steady demand. What cap do you set, and on what argument?**
   A. 6 slots, matching the modelled demand of the largest tenant so capacity is allocated proportionally to consumption
   B. A cap larger than the modelled demand but well below the pool — 16 of 64, leaving 48 slots for the other 399 tenants — because the byte-share figure models proportional appetite while the incident is a backfill that consumes everything available, and the population the cap protects is the tail
   C. 64 slots, since capping below the pool size wastes capacity that is idle most of the time
   D. No cap, and instead alert when one tenant exceeds 50% of pool utilisation so an operator can intervene
   Answer: B — The byte-share estimate is a floor, not a forecast: it describes a tenant querying in proportion to its data, and the event that actually causes an incident is one tenant reprocessing two years of history, which is not proportional to anything. So the cap is sized from the consequence rather than from the estimate — the head tenant's worst hour must still leave the tail able to plan. Setting the cap at the modelled demand starves the head tenant on any legitimate spike and generates a support ticket a week. A cap equal to the pool is not a cap, and the desk says so explicitly. An alert with a human in the loop is a detection control where a capacity control is needed: by the time an operator reads it, the tail — whose queries are mostly footer reads — has already been queueing, and those are the customers least able to absorb it.

**On Zipf as the default assumption.** The exponent that generates the population above is 1.1, which is typical of real tenant sets, and the same shape governs partition sizes and join-key frequencies — which is why → `C6.L4`'s skew argument and this lesson's capacity argument are the same argument. If you want the general theory, the **heavy-tailed distributions** literature is the place; if you want the operational version, read any multi-tenant SaaS capacity post-mortem, where the finding is invariably that the plan was built on a mean and the incident was caused by a member of the head.

**On isolation as a lattice rather than a switch.** The five boundary strengths in `tenancy-desk` — row-filter, partition, table, schema, account — are an ordering, and the useful discipline from security engineering is to ask *what enforces this* at each level. The **reference monitor** concept is the right frame: a boundary is only as strong as the component that cannot be bypassed. A predicate added by an application is not a reference monitor; a row-level security policy evaluated by the engine is closer; a separate account is one by construction. Read your platform's row-level-security documentation specifically for what happens when a query is issued by a service account with elevated rights, because that is where claimed boundaries usually turn out to be conventions.

**On metadata as the first limit.** The **Iceberg spec**'s manifest and manifest-list structure is where the per-file cost is visible, and reading it against this lesson makes the tail's 9.5× obvious rather than surprising — planning cost scales with the file list. → `tablespace T0.L2` gives the random-versus-sequential cost model underneath it, and `capacity-desk`'s `first_limit` check exists because the answer is so reliably the catalog rather than the disk.

**On the noisy neighbour.** Everything useful here came from operating systems and networks decades before it came to warehouses: **fair queueing**, **weighted fair queueing** and **admission control**. The transferable result is that fairness must be enforced at the scheduler and cannot be achieved by asking clients to behave — and that the right unit is a share of a resource, expressed as a count.

**What this lesson deliberately did not do.** It did not rank the five models. Each one is correct for some population: a hundred thousand tiny tenants cannot be table-per-tenant at any price, and four enterprise tenants under contractual isolation cannot be a shared table at any price. What the desk grades is whether your claimed boundary exists, whether cost can be attributed, whether you sized from the head, and whether the tail can still plan a query.
