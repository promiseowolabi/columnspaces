# A1.L7 — Build or Buy, Computed

_Track A1: Solution Architecture · ~22 min · columnspaces_

> Every build-or-buy memo in this industry is lost on the same line item: infrastructure gets quoted precisely and the engineer gets quoted as "we will absorb it". Here is the version with labour in engineer-months — and the ending that makes it survive a vendor.
Three shapes are on the table: a **managed warehouse**, an **engine on your own storage**, and an **appliance-class platform**. The horizon is 36 months, because that is where the term everyone underquotes stops looking like a rounding error.

The number to lead with, in the only unit that makes the comparison honest:

```text
                       build   ongoing            = labour   + migration   total
                       (EM)    (FTE × 36 mo)        (EM)       (EM)        (EM)
managed warehouse       4      0.4 → 14.4           18.4        8          26.4
engine on your storage 12      1.5 → 54.0           66.0       10          76.0
appliance platform      6      0.6 → 21.6           27.6        9          36.6
```

**EM is engineer-months. This lesson never states a price, and neither should your memo** — it states counts, and the counts are multiplied by *your* rate card, which is the reader's to own. That is not squeamishness: infrastructure rates differ by provider, region, commitment and year, but the ratio that decides every build-or-buy argument is the weight of one engineer-month against the platform rates, and that ratio varies by company and geography more than any infrastructure rate does.

Now read the table for the shape rather than the totals. The self-operated option's *build* is 12 engineer-months against the managed option's 4 — a difference of 8, which is the number that gets argued about in the room. Its *ongoing* is 54 against 14.4 — a difference of 39.6, which usually does not get argued about at all, because it arrives as 1.5 FTE on an org chart rather than as a line in a business case. **A build cost amortises: 12 engineer-months spread over 36 is 0.33 a month and then it is over. An on-call rotation does not amortise; it recurs, and it grows with the platform it operates.**

Whether labour is literally half the total is a property of your rate card, and the desk computes it and prints the share back to you. What is not rate-card-dependent is the gap: **49.6 engineer-months between the two ends of that table, which is four engineers for a year.** If you cannot find four engineers, the arithmetic has already made the decision and the rest of the memo is documentation.

The platform side is counted too, in units that survive a repricing:

```text
storage   180 TB in month 1, +3%/month, 36 months
          geometric sum = 180 × ((1.03^36 − 1) ÷ 0.03) ≈ 180 × 63.3
          ≈ 11.4 PB-months
compute   40,000 units in month 1, +2%/month
          40,000 × ((1.02^36 − 1) ÷ 0.02) ≈ 40,000 × 52 ≈ 2.08e6 compute units
egress    only on exit: 180 TB moved once
```

A compute unit is an instance-hour or a credit — **a count either way**, which is the discipline that makes this model portable. Multiply by your own four rates and you have a total. The model is the deliverable; the rates are yours.

## What each shape is bad at, and which pricing shape it lands on

A memo with no named loser per option is a recommendation wearing analysis. Every one of these three starves something:

**Managed warehouse** — lowest labour, fastest to production, and the shape is *consumption*: per byte scanned or per credit. What it is bad at: **a single bad query is a bill**, and nobody notices until invoicing, so the cost control is organisational rather than technical (quotas, pools, per-query guardrails — → C0.L5). Your layout work converts directly into money, which is pleasant, and your exit cost is the highest in the table because both the data and the semantics live inside the product.

**Engine on your own storage** — data in an open format, a second engine can read it, so the exit is the cheapest here at 4 engineer-months and near-zero egress. That number is your renewal leverage and it is the strongest commercial argument in the memo. What it is bad at: **everything is now yours** — compaction, expiry, the catalog, upgrades, the page at 3 a.m., and the maintenance jobs C3.L5 counts as three separate jobs. That is where the 1.5 FTE comes from, and quoting 0.5 there is how this option wins on a slide and loses on an invoice.

**Appliance-class platform** — predictable, no per-query anxiety, and the shape is *capacity*: you buy the platform up front. What it is bad at: **the ceiling is a wall, and you meet it on a Tuesday.** Efficiency gains produce headroom rather than a refund, so the metric to report to finance is utilisation rather than bytes scanned, and the capacity decision has a lead time that a consumption platform does not.

The pricing *shape* is not a detail; it decides which optimisation pays. Under consumption-bytes, the pruning work in C2 is literally a financial forecast. Under instance or capacity, the same work postpones a purchase and shows up as utilisation. Bring the wrong metric to the room and you will be technically right and commercially irrelevant.

---

- **49.6 EM** — labour gap between the ends of the table, over 36 months (76.0 engineer-months for the self-operated option against 26.4 for the managed one. Four engineers for a year. If they do not exist, the arithmetic has already decided.)
- **54 of 66** — engineer-months that are ongoing rather than build (1.5 FTE × 36 months. The build term amortises to 0.33 engineer-months a month and then stops; the rotation recurs and grows with the platform.)
- **28.8 EM** — what a wrong ops assumption costs, and the falsifier it gives you (If the appliance needs 1.4 FTE instead of the 0.6 assumed, that is 0.8 × 36 = 28.8 additional engineer-months — enough to close the gap to the self-operated option from 32.4 to 3.6 and reverse the recommendation. Write that sentence in the memo.)
- **4 EM + 0 egress** — exit cost of the open-format option — your renewal leverage (A vendor's pricing power is bounded by your switching cost, so the exit count is the cap on any increase they can make stick. Compute it before you sign, not during the renewal.)

## The five checks, and why `honest_answer` is the one that teaches

The TCO desk grades five things. Four of them are omissions, and each omission flatters exactly one option — which is why they are checks rather than advice.

**1 · `total`** — your horizon total for the option you recommend, within tolerance of the reference the model computes from your own counts. Itemised in counts: byte-months, compute units, engineer-months split into one-off and ongoing, migration engineer-months. If you cannot itemise it, you did not compute it.

**2 · `ongoing_ops`** — an operations term, and a *positive* one. This is the term that decides the comparison and the term routinely dropped. The desk fails a submission that omits it, and it also fails a submission that claims to have counted it while carrying **zero FTE**: no option runs itself, because a managed service still needs someone owning the schema, the upgrades and the page. A zero here is an omission dressed as a number, and it silently removes years of engineer-months from the comparison.

**3 · `migration`** — the cost of *getting there*, charged entirely to the option you are recommending. A1.L6's decomposition is the input: engineer-months plus months of parallel running. Leaving it out flatters the change and understates the status quo, which is the single most common bias in a platform memo. Zero migration engineer-months asserts that the tables, pipelines, dashboards and access model all move for free.

**4 · `exit_costed`** — the count that is both your risk if this decision is wrong and the **cap on any increase a vendor can make stick at renewal**. It is the most useful figure in the memo and the most frequently missing, because nobody wants to write it down on the day they sign. An exit of zero claims the data, the semantics *and* the operational knowledge are all portable; if you believe that, the number to write down is what makes it true.

**5 · `honest_answer`** — and this one is worth reading twice, because people misunderstand what it grades. **It has no opinion about which option is right.** It checks one thing: does the recommendation survive *your own* arithmetic? If the option you recommend is more expensive than the cheapest by more than a 5% tie band, the memo must name a non-cost reason — residency, control, latency, a skills position — and a stated reason is accepted at face value. Recommend the more expensive option all day; recommend it *silently* and the arithmetic was decoration.

That check is a professional standard rendered as a predicate. It fails the memo whose numbers point one way and whose conclusion points the other, and it passes the memo that says: *"this is not the cheapest option; it costs about 38% more over three years, and we are choosing it because the residency requirement is non-negotiable."* Both are decisions. Only one of them is a decision you can defend twelve months later.

Steps:
1. Three shapes rather than three products, because the shape is what is durable: consumption bills you for what you read, instance for what you provision, capacity for the platform up front — and each one rewards a different optimisation.
2. Count the platform side in units that survive a repricing: byte-months from a geometric sum over the horizon, compute units as instance-hours or credits, and egress bytes which appear only when you leave.
3. Then count labour in engineer-months and split it, because the split is the whole argument: a build is a one-off that amortises to a third of an engineer-month per month, while an on-call rotation recurs for every one of the thirty-six.
4. Multiply by the reader's own four rates rather than by a published price, because the only ratio that decides a build-or-buy argument is the weight of an engineer-month against the platform rates, and that is local to the company.
5. Now the four omissions the desk refuses to pass, each of which flatters exactly one option — and note that claiming a term while entering zero fails as well, because no platform operates itself and no migration is free.
6. The fifth check has no opinion about which option is right: it asks whether your recommendation survives your own arithmetic, and a more expensive choice passes as long as the non-cost reason is stated rather than implied.
7. And the memo is not finished until it says what a proof-of-concept would measure and which result would reverse it — in this model, an ops term of 1.4 FTE instead of 0.6 is enough to reverse it, and that is a sentence the vendor cannot argue with.

> **[warning]** The desk fails a zero as hard as an omission, and the reason is worth internalising: a zero is not the absence of a claim, it is a strong claim stated quietly.
> 
> **`ongoingOpsFte: 0`** claims nobody owns the schema, the upgrade, the access model or the page. On a managed platform the honest figure is small and non-zero — someone still gets called. Over 36 months, the difference between 0 and 0.4 FTE is 14.4 engineer-months, which is more than the entire build cost of the option it flatters.
> 
> **`migrationEngineerMonths: 0`** claims the tables, the 40 pipelines, the 120 dashboards and the access model all move for free. A1.L6 priced that at about 32 engineer-months of human work against 2 of data movement. This is the least believable line in a build-or-buy memo and the most common.
> 
> **`exitEngineerMonths: 0` and `exitEgressBytes: 0`** claim the data is portable, the semantics are portable *and* the operational knowledge is portable. Open formats genuinely make the first cheap and can make the second cheap; the third is never free. If you believe the exit is near-zero, write down what makes it true — a second engine you have actually run against the same files, a named alternative, a rehearsal — because that evidence is your negotiating position.
> 
> **`perEgressByte: 0`** in the rate card is the quietest of the four, and it is a claim about your contract rather than about physics. Check it rather than defaulting it.
> 
> One more, which the desk cannot see: **the 5% tie band.** If two options land within it, cost has not decided anything, and a memo that presents a 3% difference as a conclusion is over-reading its own precision. When the totals tie, say so and decide on the non-cost axes — exit, skills, residency, lead time — deliberately.

## The ending that makes it a memo rather than an opinion

Here is the constraint this course refuses to hide: **the deep-dive platform cannot be run here.** There is no cluster behind these lessons, so no figure in this course is a measured result on it. That constraint has exactly one honest ending, and it is the same ending a good architect brings to any vendor evaluation: **say what a proof-of-concept would measure, and what result would change the recommendation.**

That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the graded thing. `the-vendor`'s `poc_undefined` is a **severity-3** objection — the room is lost if it fires and you have no surviving answer — and the account executive's line is uncomfortably realistic: *"Most teams tell me 'we will run some queries and see how it feels', and then we both spend six weeks producing a result nobody can act on."*

So the memo's last section is five measurements and one reversal, all on your data and your query mix:

1. **Bytes read per query** on your top 20 queries by frequency and top 5 by bytes — a count, comparable against the scan budget you already computed (C0.L5), not a benchmark suite's query mix on someone else's distribution.
2. **p95 at your real concurrency**, taken from your own capacity plan rather than from a single-query trial. This is the one place a clock is legitimate, because latency is a *requirement*; every cost figure stays a count.
3. **The update path under your actual change rate and locality** — A1.L5's measurement: files or chunks touched, bytes written per byte of change, and how that curve behaves against a year-old row versus one written this hour. This is the diagnostic measurement, because it is the reason you are in the room.
4. **Storage held ÷ live bytes across a fortnight of restatement**, sampled daily. If reclamation is deferred rather than eliminated, this ratio is where the deferred work becomes visible.
5. **What background work the platform performs on your behalf, and which metric would move first if it fell behind.** If the answer is "there is none", ask what you would watch anyway — because this is the measurement that either validates or destroys the 0.6 FTE assumption the whole recommendation rests on.

Then the reversal, stated in the memo's own arithmetic — and this is the sentence that wins the room:

> "The recommendation rests on this platform needing about 0.6 of an engineer to operate. If the POC shows it needs 1.4, that is 0.8 × 36 = **28.8 additional engineer-months**, the labour gap against running the engine ourselves closes from 32.4 to 3.6, and the recommendation reverts. Separately, if the update path does not beat what we measured on our current stack at our own change locality, the main reason we are here disappears and we decline."

Two falsifiers, both computed from numbers already in the document, both stated before the trial. That is what `pocDefined`, `pocMetrics` and `pocFalsifier` are, and it is why `the-vendor`'s closing line is *"you have told me exactly what would make you say no, which means if you say yes I will believe it."*

Three smaller things the same room asks for, so bring them in the same section. **Name your measured first bottleneck** — storage, compute, catalog, network or ingest — because `workload_fit_unexamined` fires on its absence and "overall performance is under pressure" means any change will look like an improvement, including new hardware. **Label vendor figures as the vendor's**, treated as an upper bound to be tested. And **arrive with the exit count**, because the AE will tell you the truth about it: the teams who know their walk-away position get better pricing, and the teams who do not, do not.

_a platform memo ≡ documents you already write_

- **a hiring business case** (Nobody accepts "we will absorb it" for a headcount request. The cost is stated in people, for a period, with what they will do and what happens if they are not funded.) ≡ **the ongoing operations term** (Identical unit, hidden by a different document: 1.5 FTE for 36 months is a hiring request, and writing it as an infrastructure choice is how it gets approved without being funded.)
- **a contract renewal negotiation** (Your leverage is bounded by your alternatives, and the party who has costed their alternatives negotiates from arithmetic rather than from tone.) ≡ **exit_costed** (Which is why the exit count belongs in the memo on the day you sign rather than in the renewal thread three years later — and why an open format is a commercial instrument, not an engineering preference.)
- **a pre-registered experiment** (The hypothesis, the metric and the threshold are recorded before data collection, precisely because afterwards every threshold you pick will look like a rationalisation.) ≡ **the POC section and its falsifier** (The same discipline, and the same payoff: a trial with a written exit criterion produces a decision, while a trial without one produces six weeks of impressions and a purchase that was already made.)

**Q1. A memo compares three platforms over 36 months. Self-operation shows the lowest total; its ops line reads "absorbed by the existing platform team". The recommendation is to self-operate. What does the TCO desk do, and why?**
   A. Passes it — self-operation is the cheapest option by the memo's own numbers, so the recommendation survives the arithmetic
   B. Fails ongoing_ops, because an operations term of zero is a strong claim stated quietly rather than an absence of a claim: over 36 months a realistic 1.5 FTE is 54 engineer-months, which is where the option's apparent advantage came from. It also undermines total, because the horizon figure was computed without the term that dominates it
   C. Fails honest_answer, because self-operation is never the correct recommendation at a 36-month horizon
   D. Passes with a warning, since existing headcount is a genuine sunk cost and does not appear in an incremental comparison
   Answer: B — This is the specific failure the desk exists to catch: infrastructure is quoted precisely, labour is quoted as "we will absorb it", and self-operation wins on the slide and loses on the invoice. Absorbed capacity is not free capacity — it is 54 engineer-months of work the team will not do instead, which is exactly how the comparison should be stated. honest_answer is not the failing check here, because the recommendation does match the memo's own totals; the numbers themselves are wrong, which is what ongoing_ops and total grade. And the desk has no view on which option is right: self-operation with a positive, defended ops term passes cleanly.

**Q2. Your own arithmetic makes the managed warehouse cheapest over 36 months. You want to recommend the appliance-class platform anyway, at about 38% more. What does honest_answer require?**
   A. A revised set of assumptions that brings the appliance total inside the 5% tie band, so the recommendation is consistent with the numbers
   B. Nothing — the desk grades the arithmetic, and the recommendation is a separate judgement outside its scope
   C. A stated non-cost reason for the more expensive choice — residency, control, latency, a skills position — which the desk accepts at face value, because a more expensive option with a stated reason is a decision and without one it is an accident
   D. A sensitivity analysis showing the conditions under which the appliance becomes cheapest
   Answer: C — The check has no opinion about which option is right; it fails only a recommendation that the learner's own numbers contradict with no reason given. Naming the reason is the whole requirement, and it converts an apparent inconsistency into a defensible trade that survives being reread twelve months later. Re-deriving the assumptions until the preferred option wins is the worst available option and the most commonly taken: it makes the arithmetic decoration and destroys the credibility of every other figure in the memo. A sensitivity analysis is genuinely useful and is not a substitute for stating why you are paying more today. And "the recommendation is out of scope" describes a document that costed three options and then decided on vibes.

   A. A standard analytical benchmark suite, so platforms are compared on equal footing against published results
   B. Bytes read per query on our own top-20 query mix, p95 at our real concurrency, and the update path under our actual change rate and locality — plus two written reversals: if the platform needs 1.4 FTE rather than the 0.6 the recommendation assumes, that is 28.8 additional engineer-months and the labour gap closes from 32.4 to 3.6; and if the update path does not beat what we measured on our current stack, the main reason we are here disappears and we decline
   C. An operational readiness trial, since the architecture already addresses our bottleneck and what remains is validating runbooks and support

   Answer: B — That scope is the survive response to a severity-3 objection: metrics on your own data and query mix, plus a result that would make you say no, stated before the trial. A standard suite is the wounded answer — it measures someone else's query mix and someone else's data distribution, and it will not surface your skew. Concluding before measuring is the fatal one, and its rebuttal is the sharpest line in the room: you are about to buy a platform on the strength of an architecture diagram, and it is the vendor's. Discounting a vendor's reference figures is also wounded rather than fatal: a margin applied to a number whose mechanism you have not reproduced is still a guess, and it assumes the difference is one of degree rather than of kind.

**On costing labour rather than gesturing at it**, the durable references are outside data engineering. **Boehm's COCOMO** work matters less for its estimates than for establishing that effort is the dependent variable worth modelling; **the DORA research programme** gives the operational-load framing that turns "who gets paged" into a measurable property of a platform; and the SRE literature's *toil* definition — manual, repetitive, automatable, scaling with service size — is exactly the right lens for the 1.5 FTE line, because it identifies which part of an ops term is expected to grow with the platform and which part is not.

**On uncertainty in a decision document**, **Hubbard's *How to Measure Anything*** is the standard reference for giving decision-makers calibrated ranges instead of false points, and its central move is the one this lesson ends on: identify the variable whose uncertainty most changes the decision, then measure that one. Here it is the ops term, and the arithmetic tells you so — 0.8 FTE of error is 28.8 engineer-months, which is larger than any other uncertainty in the model.

**On lock-in as a computable quantity rather than a mood**, read your table format's specification for what actually leaves with the data. Iceberg's spec is explicit that metadata is a tree of files a second engine can read, which is why the open-format row of that table carries a 4-engineer-month exit rather than a 10-to-12-month one. Then read your contract for egress, because a rate card entry of zero there is a claim about a commercial agreement rather than about physics.

**And on the honest ending**, note what `the-vendor` is really testing. Not scepticism — the fatal response in that room is the *credulous* one, but the wounded responses are all forms of hedging. What survives is specificity: a measurement on your data, a threshold written before the trial, and a walk-away position expressed as a count. That combination is rarer in real evaluations than it should be, and it is the single most transferable thing in this track.

**A1 in one paragraph.** You went from a requirement to a reference architecture, wrote the layout design and named what it starves, sized it, made many uneven tenants share one platform, priced freshness against file count and CDC against change locality, planned a migration whose real cost is the parallel run and the human work, and turned all of it into a three-shape comparison with labour in engineer-months and a falsifiable POC at the end. Four artifacts leave with you, and the rooms attack them as predicates over the numbers you actually wrote.
