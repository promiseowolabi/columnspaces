# C5.L3 — Merge-on-Read versus Copy-on-Write

_Track C5: The Write Path · ~22 min · columnspaces_

> Thirty compaction policies, priced against the same seeded commit stream. Not one of them minimises both amplifications. Four of the thirty hold a read ceiling and a write budget at once — and on the update-heavy workload, none do, which is the most useful result in the lab.
You now have both halves of the design space. Rewrite the file (copy-on-write) or write a note beside it and let readers apply it (merge-on-read). The decision is not a preference and it is not "it depends" — it is arithmetic over two inputs you already have: **a read/write ratio** and **a freshness requirement.**

Start with bytes moved per period, using the two cost models from C5.L1. Let `F` be the size of a file a scattered update touches, `c` the bytes one commit's worth of change actually contains, `T` the number of uncompacted commits your policy tolerates, and `W` and `R` the writes and reads in the period:

```text
copy-on-write   bytes  ≈  2 × W × F        (read the file, write the file)
                reads pay nothing extra

merge-on-read   bytes  ≈  W × c            at write time
                       +  R × (T/2) × c    every read pays the mean delta

equal when      2 × W × F  =  R × (T/2) × c
                R / W      =  4F / (T × c)
```

Put real numbers in it. A 512 MB file, a commit carrying 24,000 rows at 64 B (about 1.5 MiB), a trigger of 8 uncompacted files:

```text
R / W  =  (4 × 512 MB) / (8 × 1.5 MiB)  ≈  170
```

**Above roughly 170 reads per write, the rewrite is cheaper in bytes. Below it, the delta is.** That is the whole decision on the cost axis, and it is worth noticing how *high* the crossover is: an analytical table with a scattered update stream needs to be read hundreds of times per update before copy-on-write pays, which is exactly why merge-on-read exists and why Iceberg's copy-on-write default is a default rather than a recommendation.

Then the second input, which can veto the first. **Copy-on-write's freshness floor is its own rewrite.** The change is not visible until the file has been read in full, written in full and committed. At 512 MB per affected file, a scattered ten-row update is gigabytes of I/O standing between the write and its visibility — so a sub-minute freshness requirement rules copy-on-write out no matter which side of 170 you are on. Freshness is a constraint, cost is an objective, and constraints are evaluated first.

Two inputs, and neither of them is an opinion about which mechanism is more modern.

## Then the part the arithmetic above hides: the policy is two dials, and they fight

The crossover treats `T` as given. It is not — it is yours, and so is the size of the files compaction produces. Those two dials are what the C5 browser lab makes you set, and it prices **30 policies** (6 triggers × 5 target file sizes) against a seeded stream of 240 commits at three write rates, reporting four counts and no clocks: read amplification, write amplification, storage held, files live.

The structural result, which the test suite asserts over the entire space rather than demonstrating at one point:

- **the target-size axis is strictly opposed** on all three workloads and at every trigger setting — read amplification falls monotonically as files get bigger, write amplification rises monotonically, without exception;
- **the trigger axis is opposed** from the reference target size upward: compacting more often lowers read amplification and raises write amplification;
- across the whole grid the two amplifications are **rank correlated between −0.82 and −0.91** depending on write rate. This is a frontier, not a dashboard with a best setting;
- and **no policy minimises both.** Not "usually not" — the sweep is checked for it, on every workload.

The two ends of the space are worth memorising, because they are the two positions people actually argue for in reviews:

| policy | read amp | write amp | files live | files opened per read |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **read-optimal** — compact every 2 files into 4Mi-row files | **1.00×** | **58.79×** | 4 | 2.2 |
| **write-optimal** — compact every 64 files into 16Ki-row files | 1.08× | **1.61×** | 348 | **241.4** |
| the lab's opening policy — every 8 files into 1Mi-row files | 1.01× | 5.33× | 9 | 7.8 |

Read the first row slowly. Getting read amplification to essentially perfect costs **writing every ingested byte 58.79 times over.** That is not a typo and it is not an artefact of the model: it is what "keep the base always compacted" means when every compaction rewrites the open base file to fold in a delta of two files. The second row is the mirror image — 1.61× on writes, and readers open **241 files on average, forever.**

The graded task pairs a **read ceiling of 1.02×** with a **write budget of 2.60×**: no more than 2% of what you read may be waste, and you may rewrite each ingested byte no more than 1.6 times on average. At the reference write rate, **4 of the 30 policies hold both** — every 2 or 4 files into 64Ki-row files, or every 8 or 16 files into 256Ki-row files. Neither single-objective optimum is among them, so you cannot pass by dragging a dial to its end. The lab opens *outside* the region on purpose: every 8 files into 1Mi-row files reads beautifully at 1.01× and misses the write budget by roughly 2×, which is the mistake this lesson is about.

## The result worth taking to work: one target size no trigger can rescue

Sweep the 16Ki-row column — 1 MiB files — at every trigger. Read amplification never comes under the ceiling: 1.073×, 1.050×, 1.052×, 1.052×, 1.061×, 1.082× as the trigger goes 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. It is the only target size in the grid with that property, and the test suite asserts there is exactly one.

The mechanism is **stranded tombstones**, and the numbers run the wrong way on purpose:

```text
target 16Ki rows      stranded tombstoned rows at the end of the run
trigger  2  files     282,149        <- compacting MOST often strands MOST
trigger  4  files     167,461
trigger  8  files     131,347
trigger 16  files      82,195
trigger 32  files      48,067
trigger 64  files      30,988
```

Here is why, and it is the sentence to carry: **a restatement against a row that has already been sealed into a base file cannot be purged by a compactor that only merges the pile.** It is read and discarded on every scan from then on. Sealing small files means sealing them *often*, so rows leave the reachable region faster, and the tombstones that arrive next land on files the compactor never touches. Compacting more aggressively accelerates the sealing, which strands more.

That is the honest answer to a real sentence people say: *"we increased compaction frequency and read amplification did not move."* It did not move because the trigger governs the pile, and the cost had already migrated into the base. The fix is not more compaction — it is a **larger target file size**, or a second maintenance pass that rewrites sealed files to purge tombstones, which is a *different job with its own budget* (C5.L5), or a layout that keeps mutable rows near each other so restatements stay in the reachable region (C2.L2's sort key, pointed at the write path).

And the lab prints its own limit rather than hiding it: because its compactor never rewrites a sealed file, **the write amplification it reports is a floor, not a forecast.** Add the tombstone-purging pass a real platform needs and writes go up while the stranded term goes down. A model that told you otherwise would be flattering you.

## The same tension, graded pass/fail: forge lab 05's opposing bands

The browser lab lets you explore the frontier. **Forge lab 05 makes you land on it.** Six checks, split exactly the way the production consequences are.

Three absolutes with zero tolerance, because each one is a silent wrong answer rather than a cost:

- **`read_your_writes`** — a read after a write sees that write, always. This is the queryable-buffer property from C5.L2 made non-negotiable.
- **`merge_ordered`** — the merged view of base and delta is one ordered, duplicate-free stream.
- **`delete_semantics`** — deletes and updates resolve to exactly one visible version per key, or none.
- and **`storm`** — 3,000 seeded mixed read/write/delete operations against a reference model that is itself audited against a full replay of the mutation log, so a harness bug cannot excuse a wrong answer.

Then **two bands, on the same seeded workload, pulling in opposite directions:**

```text
read_amp_bounded    levels probed per read, averaged      band ends at 6.0
write_amp_budget    rows rewritten per row ingested       band ends at 5.0

never compacting     ≈ 34.0 read   /  0.0 write    -> fails read_amp_bounded
compacting always    ≈  1.0 read   / ~base size    -> fails write_amp_budget
```

Both bands are counts, both are charged from state the harness reads off your store — `runs`, `base`, `compactions` — never from a number your code reports about itself. And the harness explains the write bill in one line worth having: every compaction rewrites the base to fold in a delta of whatever size you allowed, so **write amplification ≈ base rows ÷ delta rows at the trigger.** Compact when the delta is a thirtieth of the base and you pay about 30×. Compact on every mutation and you pay the size of the base, every time, for one row of new data.

**Passing both bands at once means you found a policy rather than expressed a preference.** That sentence is the deliverable of this entire track, and it is why the two checks were designed to be unsatisfiable by taste.

> **[warning]** Switch the browser lab to the **flood** workload — a CDC stream where nearly half of every commit restates rows that are already there — and sweep all thirty policies. The number that hold read ≤ 1.02× and write ≤ 2.60× is **zero**.
> 
> That is not a failure of the search. It is the most valuable output the lab produces, because it is a result you can act on and a shrug is not:
> 
> - **the constraint set is wrong.** If the business needs this write rate, then 1.02× read amplification is not available at 2.60× write amplification, and somebody has to choose which number moves. Say which, and by how much.
> - **or the physical layout is wrong.** Restatements land on sealed files because the rows they target are cold and scattered. Cluster by mutability instead — recent or open records together — and the tombstones stay in the region compaction can reach. That is C2's sort key aimed at the write path rather than the read path.
> - **or the table is wrong.** A stream with 45% restatements may be a change log rather than a table. Land the log, and materialise the current state on a schedule the readers actually need. This is the answer that removes the problem rather than tuning it, and it is usually the cheapest.
> - **or the platform is wrong**, which is the only case where a different architecture is the honest answer — and now you can say *which measured constraint* it has to beat instead of asking a vendor to be faster. That is the difference between an evaluation and a procurement.
> 
> The trickle workload is worth a look too: **2 of 30** policies hold both. Same physics, different pressure. So "which compaction policy should we use" has no answer that survives a change in write rate, and any runbook that states one without stating the write rate it was chosen for has documented a coincidence.

_fig 3 — two inputs pick the mechanism; two dials pick the policy; and one column of the space cannot be rescued_

Components: input 1 · reads per write (crossover at R/W ≈ 4F ÷ (T × c) ≈ 170 here) · input 2 · freshness required (a constraint, evaluated before any cost) · 30 policies priced (6 triggers × 5 target sizes, one seeded stream) · copy-on-write: reads pay 1.00× (writes pay the whole file, and the rewrite IS the freshness floor) · merge-on-read: writes pay the change (every read pays the delta until compaction folds it away) · the two ends of the space: read-optimal is 1.00× read at 58.79× write · write-optimal is 1.61× write at 1.08× read and 241 files opened per read (rank correlated −0.82 to −0.91 across the grid, and NO policy minimises both — checked over the whole sweep, on every workload) · the dual constraint: read ≤ 1.02× AND write ≤ 2.60× (4 of 30 hold both at the reference rate · 2 of 30 on trickle · 0 of 30 on flood) · 16Ki rows: no trigger reaches the ceiling (stranded tombstones — and compacting MORE often strands MORE of them) · so the deliverable is a policy with both numbers stated, plus the write rate it was chosen for — and forge lab 05 grades the same pair as opposing bands (read_amp_bounded ≤ 6.0 probes per read against write_amp_budget ≤ 5.0 rows rewritten per row ingested, on one workload, from state read off your store)

Steps:
1. Input one is the read/write ratio, and the crossover is arithmetic rather than judgement: at a 512 MB file, a 1.5 MiB commit and a trigger of 8, the rewrite only becomes the cheaper option above about 170 reads per write.
2. Input two is freshness, and it is a constraint rather than an objective: copy-on-write cannot make a change visible until it has read and rewritten every affected file, so a sub-minute target rules it out on scattered updates.
3. Choosing merge-on-read only starts the real decision, so the lab prices every policy the reader can express — six compaction triggers by five target file sizes — against one seeded commit stream at three write rates.
4. And the two ends of that space are the two positions people argue for in reviews: essentially perfect reads bought with 58.79× writes, or 1.61× writes bought with 241 files opened on every single query, forever.
5. Which is why the graded task is a pair rather than a preference: hold a read ceiling and a write budget together, satisfied by four of thirty policies here, two on the trickle rate, and none at all when half the commits are restatements.
6. One column of the grid cannot be rescued by any trigger, because restatements against already-sealed small files strand tombstones the compactor never reaches — and sealing more often strands more of them, not fewer.
7. So what ships is not a mechanism but a policy with both amplifications stated and the write rate they were chosen for, which is exactly the pair forge lab 05 grades as opposing bands on a single workload.

- **4 of 30** — policies holding read ≤ 1.02× and write ≤ 2.60× (At the reference write rate. Two of thirty at the trickle rate and none at all on the update-heavy flood workload, which is the result that changes a requirement rather than a setting.)
- **58.79×** — write amplification of the read-optimal policy (Compacting every 2 files into 4Mi-row files gets read amplification to 1.00× by writing every ingested byte 58.79 times over. The mirror policy costs 1.08× reads at 1.61× writes.)
- **241 files** — opened per read by the write-optimal policy (Mean files a single read must open, with 348 live at the end of the run. Cheap writes are paid for in file count, which is the cost that scales planning rather than bytes.)
- **−0.82 to −0.91** — rank correlation between the two amplifications (Across all 30 policies, on all three write rates. Strongly negative means the space itself trades one for the other; a value near zero would mean the lab was teaching a tradeoff its model does not have.)

> **[vendor snapshot — verified 2026-09]** In Iceberg this decision is three table properties, and the default is the rewrite
>
> Iceberg exposes exactly the decision this lesson makes, per operation, as table configuration:
> 
> | property | default | documented values |
> |---|---|---|
> | `write.delete.mode` | `copy-on-write` | copy-on-write or merge-on-read (v2 and above) |
> | `write.update.mode` | `copy-on-write` | copy-on-write or merge-on-read (v2 and above) |
> | `write.merge.mode` | `copy-on-write` | copy-on-write or merge-on-read (v2 and above) |
> | `write.delete.granularity` | `partition` | partition or file |
> | `write.delete.target-file-size-bytes` | 67108864 (64 MB) | against 536870912 (512 MB) for data files |
> | `write.delete.isolation-level` | `serializable` | serializable or snapshot |
> 
> Four things to take from that table, all of them architectural rather than perishable.
> 
> **The choice is per operation, not per table.** A table can rewrite on `DELETE` and merge on `MERGE`, which is usually what you want: the erasure path and the upsert path have different read/write ratios and different freshness requirements, so running the crossover arithmetic once for the whole table is running it wrong.
> 
> **The default is the rewrite.** Every table you inherit is copy-on-write on all three paths unless someone changed it, which means the amplification C3.L4 measured is the default behaviour of the ecosystem's most-used format rather than an edge case.
> 
> **Delete files get a smaller target size than data files** — 64 MB against 512 MB — which is a statement about their expected lifetime: they are meant to be folded away, not accumulated. Treat that as a hint about your compaction cadence rather than as a number to tune.
> 
> **Granularity is the stranded-tombstone dial, named.** `partition` granularity produces fewer, wider delete files; `file` granularity scopes each delete to one data file, which is what lets a compaction rewrite one data file and drop the delete with it. The spec's v3 deletion vectors take that to its conclusion: at most one deletion vector per data file per snapshot, stored as a Puffin blob, introduced because it is more efficient at execution time than position delete files. Delta arrives at the same structure with the same Roaring bitmap format — and when two independent specifications converge on a structure, the structure is the physics.
> 
> Re-check the constants against your version; the shape — three modes, a copy-on-write default, and delete files sized to be temporary — is durable.
>
> Sources: https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/configuration/ · https://iceberg.apache.org/spec/

_the two amplifications ≡ tradeoffs you have already made this way_

- **index maintenance versus scan cost** (Every index you add makes some reads cheaper and every write more expensive. Nobody asks whether indexes are good; they ask how many, on which columns, and what the write path can afford.) ≡ **compaction frequency** (The same question with the same shape and one difference: here the reader pays continuously until maintenance runs, so the cost of deferring is charged to whoever is querying rather than to whoever is writing.)
- **a garbage collector's throughput/pause trade** (Collect often and you pay steady overhead; collect rarely and you pay a large stall. There is no setting that is best on both, so tuning means choosing a budget and monitoring for the day the workload leaves it.) ≡ **the trigger and target-size dials** (Measured here as a frontier with rank correlation between −0.82 and −0.91: not one of the thirty policies wins on both axes, so the deliverable is a stated budget rather than an optimum.)
- **the RUM conjecture** (Read overhead, update overhead, memory overhead — an access method may optimise two and must pay on the third. It is a framing device for why no structure is universally best.) ≡ **this lab, made concrete** (Columnar storage picks read and memory, and merge-on-read is how it buys back some update performance. The four-of-thirty result is the conjecture with numbers attached and a workload named.)

**Q1. A table gets about 400 scattered single-row updates a day and is read by roughly 20 dashboard queries an hour. Files average 512 MB, commits carry about 1.5 MiB of change, and the freshness requirement is 5 minutes. Which analysis do you present?**
   A. Copy-on-write, because analytics tables are read-heavy and merge-on-read is for streaming systems
   B. Merge-on-read, on both inputs: at ~480 reads and ~400 writes a day the read/write ratio is near 1, far below the ~170 crossover where rewriting whole files becomes the cheaper option, and the 5-minute freshness target cannot survive a multi-gigabyte rewrite standing between the write and its visibility. Then set the trigger and target size against a stated read ceiling and write budget
   C. Merge-on-read, because it avoids write amplification entirely and therefore has no ongoing cost
   D. Either — the difference is small at this scale, so pick whichever the platform defaults to and revisit if the bill moves
   Answer: B — Both inputs point the same way and both should be stated. The crossover 4F ÷ (T × c) is around 170 reads per write here, and the actual ratio is near 1, so copy-on-write would move hundreds of gigabytes to change 400 rows. The freshness constraint decides it independently: a rewrite of every affected 512 MB file has to complete before the change is visible, which no 5-minute target survives on scattered updates. "Analytics tables are read-heavy" is folklore that skips the arithmetic — read-heavy has a threshold and this table is nowhere near it. Merge-on-read does not avoid write amplification: the lab measures 1.61× at the cheapest policy in the grid, and readers pay the delta continuously. And deferring to the default is how tables end up copy-on-write with a compliance workload attached, since copy-on-write is precisely what the format defaults to.

**Q2. Read amplification on a merge-on-read table is above your ceiling. An engineer tightens the compaction trigger from 16 files to 2 — eight times more compaction. Read amplification barely moves and the storage bill rises. What happened, and what actually fixes it?**
   A. Compaction is failing silently; check for commit conflicts, since a compaction that loses its race with a writer retries and may be giving up
   B. The cost had already migrated out of the delta pile and into sealed base files as stranded tombstones — restatements against rows already sealed cannot be purged by a compactor that only merges the pile, and sealing more often strands more of them. At a 1 MiB target the lab measures 282,149 stranded rows at a trigger of 2 against 30,988 at a trigger of 64. The fixes are a larger target file size, a separate pass that rewrites sealed files to purge tombstones with its own budget, or clustering mutable rows together
   C. The trigger only affects newly written files, so the improvement will appear once the existing backlog ages out
   D. Read amplification is dominated by footer reads, so the fix is to disable statistics collection on delta files and reduce metadata volume
   Answer: B — This is the counter-intuitive result the lab is built to produce, and it is measured rather than argued: at the smallest target size, every trigger setting fails the read ceiling, and the stranded-tombstone count runs the wrong way — the most aggressive trigger strands nearly ten times as many rows as the laziest one, because sealing small files often pushes rows out of the region a compactor can reach. So more compaction spends write amplification and storage on a term it cannot touch. Commit conflicts are a real failure mode and would show as failed compactions rather than as ineffective ones. Waiting for a backlog to age out misdiagnoses a structural term as a transient one. And removing statistics from delta files makes pruning worse while leaving the discarded rows exactly where they are.

**Q3. Your ingest becomes a CDC stream where about 45% of commits restate existing rows. Sweeping all 30 policies, none holds read ≤ 1.02× and write ≤ 2.60×. What is the professional response?**
   A. Keep the constraints and search harder — the grid is coarse, so a trigger or target size between the sampled values will satisfy both
   B. Report that the region is empty and treat that as the finding: at this write rate those two numbers are not simultaneously available, so either a constraint moves (and you say which, and to what), or the layout changes so restatements stay in the compactable region, or the stream is landed as a change log with current state materialised on a schedule the readers need. If a different platform is the answer, you can now name the measured constraint it has to beat
   C. Relax the write budget to whatever the read-optimal policy costs, since read amplification is what users experience and write amplification is only background work
   D. Raise the read ceiling to the write-optimal policy's value, since 1.08× is close enough to 1.02× that the difference is unlikely to matter
   Answer: B — An empty feasible region is information, not a search failure, and reporting it is the whole point of having priced the space: it converts "compaction is expensive" into "these two numbers are not jointly achievable at this write rate", which a room can act on. Two of the alternatives silently pick a winner — one abandons the write budget (the read-optimal policy costs 58.79× writes, a standing background bill), the other abandons the read ceiling by asserting the gap is small without saying what it costs at your query volume. Both may be the right decision; neither is acceptable undeclared. Interpolating between grid points is the least defensible option: the axes are monotone, so a value between two failing settings fails too, and the emptiness is caused by the workload rather than by grid resolution.

The frontier this lesson measures has been studied properly, and not in the data-lake literature.

Start with **O'Neil, Cheng, Gawlick and O'Neil, "The Log-Structured Merge-Tree" (1996)**, which is where "accept the write cheaply and merge later" was first analysed as a cost model rather than described as a technique. Then **Dayan, Athanassoulis and Idreos, "Monkey: Optimal Navigable Key-Value Store" (SIGMOD 2017)** and **"Dostoevsky" (SIGMOD 2018)**: the contribution to steal is the framing of *tiering versus levelling* as a continuum with a tunable knob, and the demonstration that the right setting depends on the read/write mix rather than on a house style. Your compaction trigger is that knob with a different name.

**Luo and Carey's survey, "LSM-based storage techniques: a survey" (VLDB Journal 2020)** is the single best map of the design space, including the parts this lab does not model: partial compaction, level-based versus size-tiered selection, and the compaction-scheduling literature.

For the columnar-specific side, **Héman et al. on Positional Delta Trees (SIGMOD 2010)** explains why applying a delta *by position* merges in one pass, which is the mechanism behind Iceberg's position deletes and its v3 deletion vectors. Read the Iceberg spec's three generations in order — position deletes, equality deletes, deletion vectors — as an evolution driven entirely by execution-time merge cost.

The cost frame remains **the RUM conjecture (Athanassoulis et al., EDBT 2016)**, and the best-calibrated vocabulary for write amplification is still the flash-endurance literature, which has been measuring media writes per host write since long before anyone compacted a data lake.

One deliberate gap in this lesson, stated so it does not read as an omission: the browser lab's compactor **never rewrites a sealed base file**, so its write amplification is a floor and its stranded-tombstone term is an upper bound on what a real platform would leave behind. A production maintenance job must eventually rewrite sealed files to purge tombstones, which raises writes and lowers reads — a second, separate budget. That job is **C5.L5**, and the desk that grades it is `compaction-desk`.

Next: **C5.L4**. You have the policy dials. Now the input variable nobody owns — the batch interval — which sets the file count, the freshness and the compaction rate you have to fund.
