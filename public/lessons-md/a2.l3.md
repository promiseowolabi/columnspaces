# A2.L3 — Automating Compaction and Lifecycle

_Track A2: Platform Engineering · ~20 min · columnspaces_

> Four background jobs decide whether your tables stay queryable and what they cost while doing nothing a user asked for. Each one gets a budget in counts — because a maintenance job without a budget is an unbounded bill with a cron entry.
C5.L5 gave you a compaction policy and a write-amplification budget for one table. This lesson is the platform version: **four jobs, running unattended across every table you own, each with a stated budget in counts and a stated symptom when it falls behind.**

The four, and why the fourth surprises people:

1. **Compaction** — rewrite small files into large ones. Holds read amplification down; costs write amplification.
2. **Snapshot expiry** — drop old snapshots so superseded files become deletable. Holds storage down; costs recovery options.
3. **Orphan cleanup** — delete files that no metadata references. Nothing else can find them, because expiry removes files a *former* snapshot referenced and these were never referenced at all (C3.L3).
4. **Metadata compaction** — rewrite manifests, and expire old table-metadata files. Holds *planning* down, and it is the one missing from most runbooks because its symptom is not storage and not correctness, it is the 4% → **61%** planning share from INC-2.

All four are the same shape of thing: a job with a trigger, a budget, an alarm and a backlog slope. And all four commit through the catalog like any other writer (A2.L1), which is why their request volume belongs in the catalog's budget rather than in a footnote.

The discipline that makes this a policy rather than a schedule: **every budget is a count, never a clock.** "Two hours of compaction nightly" means a different amount of work on every cluster size, cannot be diffed between weeks, and cannot be held to a limit. The compaction desk grades a wall-clock cost statement as a failure for exactly that reason.

## One worked policy, all four jobs, every number redoable

Take the compaction desk's reference table: **40 GiB live**, 2 GiB of logical change a day, a levelled compactor that rewrites **3%** of the base per run, a trigger at **512 MiB** of accumulated delta, **128 MiB** target files, a reader that opens **22 files** with **24 KiB** footers to answer a query needing **32 MiB** of live data, **3%** of the base currently tombstoned, and a **7-day** expiry window.

**Job 1 — compaction.**

```text
write amp   = 1 + (base × scope) ÷ delta-at-trigger
            = 1 + (40 GiB × 0.03) ÷ 512 MiB
            = 1 + 1,228.8 MiB ÷ 512 MiB  =  3.4×

runs/day    = 2 GiB ÷ 512 MiB            =  4
rewritten   = 4 × 40 GiB × 0.03          =  4.8 GiB/day  =  12% of the table, daily
files out   = ceil(4.8 GiB ÷ 128 MiB)    =  39/day
files in    = 39 + ceil(2 GiB ÷ 128 MiB) =  55/day
requests    = 39 + 55                    =  94/day       (PUTs + GETs, the C0.L3 term)
```

Say the budget as a bill and not as a ratio: **every ingested byte is written 3.4 times, forever, for as long as this table exists.** Alarm the share of table rewritten per day, not the absolute bytes — above roughly **20%/day** you are rewriting the whole table every five days to service an ingest configuration, and the ingest configuration is what to change (C5.L4).

**What that buys, itemised — because a read-amp figure with a term missing is the norm:**

```text
data term      = 1 + 512 MiB ÷ (2 × 40 GiB)      = 1.00625
metadata term  = 22 files × 24 KiB ÷ 32 MiB      = 0.01611
read amp       =                                   1.0224×
```

**The metadata term is 2.6× the data term.** Footers, not data. Which means at this file size lowering the trigger buys you almost nothing and the lever is fewer, larger files. Quote read amplification averaged over the whole trigger cycle rather than at the end of it, or you are grading where the last compaction happened to fall.

**Job 2 — snapshot expiry, at 7 days.** Retained superseded bytes are 4.8 GiB/day × 7 = **33.6 GiB**, so storage is live 40 + tombstoned 1.2 + retained 33.6 = **74.8 GiB, a multiple of 1.87× live.** Then the sentence that makes it a decision instead of a setting: *past seven days, recovery is restore-from-backup, not time travel.* With no window at all, this component has no bound — the desk projects it against your horizon and marks storage unbounded rather than printing infinity, and it costs an extra half an engineer-month a year because somebody has to reason about storage growth by hand.

**Job 3 — orphan cleanup, weekly.** Its cost is requests, not bytes: it has to enumerate storage and diff it against the metadata tree. At 1.5M objects and 1,000 keys per LIST page that is **~1,500 LIST requests per sweep** plus the tree read. Its budget is a count of requests and a cadence; its safety parameter is a retention interval that **must exceed your longest in-flight write** (see the vendor block — shortening it can delete files belonging to a commit in progress).

**Job 4 — metadata compaction.** At a 5-minute commit interval, one table produces **288 table-metadata files and 288 manifest lists a day**, about **105,000 metadata files a year**, and the default settings track the last hundred and delete none of the rest. Its triggers are manifest size and manifest count per snapshot; its symptom is planning share, and planning share is the metric that went from 4% to 61% while every other dashboard looked healthy.

---

- **3.4×** — write amplification: every ingested byte written 3.4 times, forever (1 + (40 GiB × 0.03) ÷ 512 MiB. State it as a bill rather than a ratio — "3.4×" sounds like a measurement, "we rewrite every byte 3.4 times" is what it is.)
- **1.0224×** — read amplification, of which the metadata term is 2.6× the data term (Data 1.00625 from unmerged delta, metadata 0.01611 from 22 footers at 24 KiB against a 32 MiB answer. At this file size the footers cost more than the delta, so the lever is fewer files rather than a lower trigger.)
- **12%/day** — of the table rewritten, producing zero rows anybody asked for (4 compactions a day × 40 GiB × 3% scope = 4.8 GiB. Alarm the share rather than the bytes; above about 20%/day you are rewriting the whole table every five days.)
- **1.87×** — storage held ÷ live at a 7-day expiry window (live 40 GiB + tombstoned 1.2 GiB + retained superseded 33.6 GiB = 74.8 GiB. Remove the window and the third component has no bound at all.)

> **[vendor snapshot — verified 2026-09]** Iceberg's own maintenance guidance — including the inversion worth arguing with
>
> The maintenance documentation splits the work into two headings, and the split is not the one most platform teams would guess.
> 
> **Recommended maintenance:** *expire snapshots*, *remove old metadata files*, *delete orphan files*.
> **Optional maintenance:** *compact data files*, *rewrite manifests*.
> 
> On expiry: *"Data files are not deleted until they are no longer referenced by a snapshot that may be used for time travel or rollback. Regularly expiring snapshots deletes unused data files."* That is the whole erasure-deadline argument in one sentence, and A2.L7 turns it into a date.
> 
> On metadata files, the defaults are the part to write down. `write.metadata.delete-after-commit.enabled` defaults to **false**; `write.metadata.previous-versions-max` defaults to **100**. The documented example: with deletion disabled and a tracking limit of 10, *"after 100 commits, one will have 10 tracked metadata files and 90 orphaned metadata files"*, and those 90 *"can only be cleaned with an orphan file deletion procedure"*. On a streaming table, that is a directory that grows monotonically by default and whose cleanup path is job 3 rather than job 2.
> 
> On orphan cleanup, two warnings worth quoting verbatim in your runbook:
> 
> - *"It is dangerous to remove orphan files with a retention interval shorter than the time expected for any write to complete because it might corrupt the table if in-progress files are considered orphaned and are deleted. The default interval is 3 days."*
> - Orphan detection compares **string representations of paths**, so if the path authority changes — the doc's example is changing authorities for an HDFS cluster — nothing in a current listing matches the metadata, and *"this will lead to data loss when RemoveOrphanFiles is run."*
> 
> That second one is a job whose failure mode is deleting live data, which puts it in a different risk class from the other three and means it needs a dry-run mode and a reviewed output, not just a schedule.
> 
> On manifests: *"The metadata tree functions as an index over a table's data. Manifests in the metadata tree are automatically compacted in the order they are added, which makes queries faster when the write pattern aligns with read filters."* Rewriting them explicitly is for when write order and query filters diverge — the doc's example rewrites manifests smaller than 10 MB and regroups by the first partition field.
> 
> **Now argue with the taxonomy, because that is the useful part.** "Optional" is accurate from the format's perspective: skip compaction and every guarantee still holds, every query still returns the right answer. It is wrong from the platform's perspective, because the thing that degrades is the cost and the planning time of every query on the table, and that is what your consumer experiences. Treat the word *optional* as meaning **"not required for correctness"**, and hold all four to a budget anyway.
>
> Sources: https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/maintenance/ · https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/configuration/

_fig 1 — four jobs, four budgets in counts, four different symptoms when one falls behind_

Components: 1 · compaction (trigger 512 MiB delta · 4 runs/day · 39 files out) · 2 · snapshot expiry (7-day window · holds 33.6 GiB superseded) · 3 · orphan cleanup (weekly · ~1,500 LIST requests per sweep) · 4 · metadata compaction (288 metadata files/day · ~105,000 a year) · write amp BUDGETED: 3.4× (4.8 GiB/day = 12% of the table, every day) · read amp BOUNDED: 1.0224× (data 1.00625 + metadata 0.01611 — footers dominate) · storage, three components, all three bounded: live 40 GiB + tombstoned 1.2 GiB + retained 33.6 GiB = 74.8 GiB (1.87× live) (remove the expiry window and the third component has no bound — bounded in ratio, unbounded in bytes) · the symptoms, in the order they appear: backlog slope → planning share → storage multiple → the invoice (planning share went 4% → 61% in INC-2 while storage, correctness and job status all looked entirely healthy) · and the cost, in counts only: bytes rewritten/day · share of table/day · created vs merged per hour · requests added · 0.25 engineer-months a year (no clocks, no prices — name the pricing shape and multiply by your own rate)

Steps:
1. Two jobs are about files: compaction merges small ones into large ones on a delta trigger, and expiry lets superseded ones become deletable after a stated window. Both are commits, so both queue behind the same catalog as your writers.
2. Two jobs are about things no query will ever find: files that no metadata references, and a metadata tree that grows by one table-metadata file and one manifest list per commit whether or not anybody reads them.
3. Compaction moves cost from the read path to the write path rather than removing it, and both halves get a number: a budget of 3.4× on writes, and a bound of 1.0224× on reads with its two terms separated.
4. Storage is only stable when all three of its components have a bound. Live data grows with the business, tombstoned bytes are bounded by the compaction trigger, and retained superseded files are bounded only by an expiry window that somebody chose on purpose.
5. The symptoms arrive in a fixed order and the invoice is last, which is why the first two belong on a dashboard: backlog slope moves first, planning share second, storage third, and the bill arrives after the month has already been spent.
6. And the whole cost is stated as counts plus a pricing shape, never as hours: an hour of compaction means different work on every cluster, so it cannot be diffed, replayed or held to a budget, and the desk grades a wall-clock statement as a failure.

> **[warning]** Compaction, expiry and metadata compaction fail *safe*: they fall behind, and things get slower and larger. Orphan cleanup fails *unsafe*, because its whole function is deleting files it believes nothing references. Three documented ways to get that wrong:
> 
> **Retention shorter than your longest write.** A commit in progress has written data files that no metadata references yet — which is the definition the sweep is matching on. Iceberg's docs state it plainly: a retention interval shorter than the time any write takes *"might corrupt the table"*, with a default of 3 days. So the parameter is not a tuning knob, it is a function of your slowest backfill. Write down that backfill's duration next to the setting.
> 
> **Path strings that stopped matching.** Orphan detection compares path representations, so a change of authority, mount, endpoint or bucket alias makes every live file look unreferenced. The doc's warning is explicit that this *"will lead to data loss"*. A dry run whose output is reviewed by a human catches it in one line: if the candidate list is approximately the entire table, the comparison is broken and not the table.
> 
> **A sweep that outruns the catalog's view.** The sweep reads the metadata tree and lists storage, and those two reads happen at different moments. If a commit lands in between, files from it are candidates. This is why the retention interval and a stable snapshot of the tree are both required, and why this job should never be the one you run "quickly, by hand, during an incident."
> 
> Runbook consequence: orphan cleanup gets a **dry-run mode, a candidate count with a sanity bound, and a named approver** for any sweep whose candidate list exceeds that bound. It is the only one of the four jobs where the correct response to an anomaly is to stop rather than to investigate while it runs.

_the four jobs ≡ maintenance you already budget somewhere else_

- **autovacuum in a row store** (Reclaims space from dead tuples, competes with user work, and its failure mode is bloat plus a transaction-id horizon that eventually stops writes entirely. Nobody calls it optional, and everybody has an alert on its backlog.) ≡ **compaction plus expiry** (The same job split in two: compaction removes the dead rows from files, expiry makes the superseded files deletable. And the same operational truth — the metric that predicts trouble is the backlog slope, not the storage graph. (→ tablespace T5 for the vacuum side in detail.))
- **log rotation with a retention policy** (Everyone accepts that retention costs storage and that deleting logs removes an investigative option. The window has an owner, a compliance input, and nobody pretends it is a storage optimisation.) ≡ **the snapshot expiry window** (Identical document, with one extra sting: the window is part of your erasure deadline (A2.L7), and it is also the thing an RPO leaning on time travel silently depends on — which is one failure domain where the plan claimed two (A2.L6).)
- **garbage collection with a safepoint** (Collectors must never free something still reachable, so they need a consistent view and a conservative margin. Aggressive tuning trades a little space for the possibility of collecting live objects, which is not a trade anyone makes twice.) ≡ **orphan cleanup and its retention interval** (Exactly the same safety argument in a distributed setting: the "still reachable" test is a diff of storage against the metadata tree, the margin is the retention interval, and the conservative default exists because an in-flight commit looks identical to garbage.)

**Q1. A platform team reports: "compaction runs nightly, storage is flat and read performance is fine." Finance later asks why the platform bill grew 30% in a quarter with no new workloads. What was missing, and why did it surface as a cost surprise rather than an incident?**
   A. A latency SLO for the maintenance window, since a nightly job that overruns collides with the morning query peak
   B. A write-amplification budget and its cost as counts — bytes rewritten per day, that as a share of table size per day, files created against files merged, requests added — because without them the rewrite volume is simply whatever the current ingest configuration implies, so a change to the batch interval multiplies a background bill that no dashboard attributes to compaction
   C. A record of the engine version running the job, since default target file sizes change between releases
   D. Nothing important: read amplification and storage are the outcomes that matter, and background work does not compete with user queries
   Answer: B — A job with a schedule and no budget is not a policy, and its cost is a dependent variable: halve the commit interval and the rewrite volume rises with nothing in the compaction configuration changing, which is exactly why the increase gets blamed on "the platform" rather than on the ingest ticket that caused it. At the worked policy, 4.8 GiB a day on a 40 GiB table is 12% of the table rewritten daily, producing zero rows a user asked for — a number that is defensible when stated in advance and indefensible when discovered in an invoice. A latency SLO is worth having and measures a clock, so it means different things on different hardware; version drift is real and small beside an unbounded rewrite volume. The last option is the belief this track exists to remove: background work is paid for in bytes, requests and capacity, and on a consumption shape it is indistinguishable from the queries it was meant to make cheaper.

**Q2. An engineer proposes running orphan cleanup daily with a 6-hour retention interval, arguing that the default of 3 days lets junk accumulate and that storage is the cheapest thing in the platform. What is the risk, and what is the correct parameter?**
   A. No real risk: orphan cleanup only deletes files no metadata references, so a shorter interval simply reclaims space sooner
   B. The interval must exceed the duration of the longest in-flight write, because a commit in progress has already written data files that no metadata references yet — which is precisely the condition the sweep matches. At 6 hours, any backfill or large rewrite running longer than that has its files deleted underneath it, and the documented outcome is a corrupted table rather than a failed job
   C. The risk is cost: daily sweeps of a large object store multiply LIST requests, so the correct answer is to keep the 3-day default purely to reduce request volume
   D. The risk is that snapshot expiry and orphan cleanup will race, so the fix is to serialise the two jobs and the interval can safely be 6 hours
   Answer: B — This is the one maintenance job whose failure mode is deleting live data, and the mechanism is that an in-flight commit is indistinguishable from garbage: files written, nothing referencing them yet. The retention interval is therefore a function of your slowest write — a backfill, a large rewrite, a manual restatement — and not a tuning preference. The first option restates the sweep's intent while ignoring the visibility gap that makes it dangerous, which is exactly the reasoning that causes the incident. Request volume is a genuine secondary cost (roughly 1,500 LIST requests per sweep at 1.5M objects and 1,000 keys per page) but it is a bill, not a corruption. Serialising with expiry addresses a different hazard and leaves the in-flight write exposed, so the interval would still be wrong.

**Q3. You submit a compaction policy claiming read amplification of 1.00× on a table where a query opens 22 files with 24 KiB footers to answer from 32 MiB of live data, with a 512 MiB trigger against a 40 GiB base. The desk fails the check. Why, and what is the defensible figure?**
   A. 1.00× is unattainable in principle, so the desk expects a conservative round-up such as 1.10× to allow for variance
   B. The claim omits the metadata term: 22 × 24 KiB ÷ 32 MiB is 0.016, and the data term from unmerged delta is only 1 + 512 MiB ÷ (2 × 40 GiB) = 1.00625 — so the reference is about 1.022×, the footers cost 2.6 times the delta, and the lever at this file size is fewer, larger files rather than a lower trigger
   C. Read amplification should be measured at the end of the trigger cycle, and 1.00× is what you get immediately after a compaction completes
   D. The figure is fine but the ceiling was not stated, and the check grades the ceiling rather than the claim
   Answer: B — Footers are read bytes like any other, and the desk itemises the two terms precisely because a read-amp figure quoted without the metadata term is the normal error — it treats planning as free. Here the metadata term is the larger half, which is the genuinely useful result: it says lowering the trigger to merge delta sooner buys almost nothing, and the design lever is file size. Option three describes the second classic error rather than a fix: measuring at the end of the cycle grades where the last compaction happened to fall, which is why the reference averages a read after every commit and produces the 1 + trigger ÷ 2·base form. And the ceiling is a separate submission from the claim — the check grades your figure against a band around the reference and separately against the ceiling you committed to, so a missing ceiling is a different failure from an under-counted claim.

---

Read **Iceberg's maintenance page and its configuration page side by side**, and write down every default that affects a count: target file size, previous-versions-max, delete-after-commit, orphan retention. Those four defaults, unexamined, are the difference between a platform whose metadata directory is bounded and one whose isn't. Then read **Delta's VACUUM and log-cleanup documentation** and notice both projects moving the *authorisation* for maintenance toward the catalog — which is the direction A2.L1 argued from first principles, and a strong signal about where this responsibility belongs in your own design.

For the theory of the trade you are making, **the RUM conjecture (Athanassoulis et al., EDBT 2016)** is the shortest correct statement: read, update and memory overheads cannot all be optimised at once, so a compaction policy is a choice of which one to spend. **Luo and Carey's LSM survey (VLDBJ 2020)** gives the policy space with names, and **Dayan and Idreos on Monkey and Dostoevsky** show tiering-versus-levelling as a tunable rather than a doctrine — which is exactly what the `compactionScopeFraction` input in the desk's model represents.

For write amplification as an operational discipline with mature vocabulary, the flash-endurance literature has been measuring media writes per host write for two decades and gave us the phrase *steady state* for precisely this situation. Borrow the vocabulary; it makes the conversation with a storage team much shorter.

And for the organisational half — a background job with an owner, a budget and an alarm — **the SRE literature on toil** is the right reference, because the number that actually decides whether this policy survives contact with reality is the one the desk quietly reports: **0.25 engineer-months a year to own it once built, rising to 0.75 with no expiry window** because somebody then has to reason about storage growth by hand. That is a staffing line, and A2.L5 puts it in a capacity plan.

Next: **A2.L4**. These four jobs have budgets now. The remaining question is how you find out they have fallen behind before your consumer does — and the answer is not job status, because the incident that actually happens has green jobs.
