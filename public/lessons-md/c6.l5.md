# C6.L5 — Elasticity and What It Costs

_Track C6: Distributed Analytics · ~16 min · columnspaces_

> Disaggregation buys independent scaling, disposable compute and one copy for many engines. It charges for all three in round trips — and the workload that pays is the one that looked fine on local disk.
C6.L4 compared the architectures on node loss and rescale. This lesson prices the design the industry actually moved to, in both directions, because a platform memo that lists only the benefits is a memo somebody will use against you in year two.

Three things disaggregation buys, and each is a count rather than an adjective.

**1 · Storage and compute scale independently.** A table growing 40% a year forces no CPU purchase, and a quarter-end reporting spike forces no storage purchase. In the coupled design those two are one procurement, which is why coupled clusters end up over-provisioned on whichever axis grew slower.

**2 · Compute becomes disposable.** If no node owns data, a node can die mid-query and be replaced, and a cluster can resize between queries. Put a count on it: a warehouse that is genuinely needed for two hours a day, at 32 workers, is

```
elastic     32 workers × 2 h   =    64 worker-hours/day
always-on   32 workers × 24 h  =   768 worker-hours/day
ratio                                12×
```

Whether you capture that 12× depends entirely on whether your workload actually has a two-hour shape and whether your platform bills in units that can go to zero — but the count is the argument, and it is the reason the industry crossed the table from left to right despite object storage being two orders of magnitude worse at latency.

**3 · One copy, many engines.** Data in an open format on shared storage is read by a warehouse engine, a Python process and a training job without a pipeline between them. The saving here is not bytes, it is **pipelines you did not build**: every copy is a schema to keep in sync, a freshness lag to explain and a divergence incident waiting to happen (C7 and A2 both live here).

That is the case for the move. Now the invoice.

---

## What it costs: round trips become a first-class resource

C0.L3 derived this and it is not re-derived here — read it if the phrase "bandwidth is a product" is not yet reflexive. The one-line version: every storage regime is characterised by **requests per second** and **bytes per request**, bandwidth is their product, and quoting only the product hides exactly the workloads that regress.

What C6 adds is what happens to that cost model when the compute is elastic:

- **The distance is now permanent.** In a shared-nothing design a scan is a local read and request size barely matters. Once storage is remote, every technique from C1 through C4 — larger encoded blocks, zone maps that avoid requests entirely, late materialization, batch execution — is partly a response to the distance, and the layout parameter that used to be about pruning (row group size, C2.L1) is now also your request size.
- **Every scale-out event starts cold.** A new worker's cache is empty, so its first queries pay full remote-read cost. Elasticity and cache warmth are in direct tension: the more aggressively you resize, the less of your working set is local when a query arrives.
- **Concurrency is bounded by requests, not cores.** This is the part that surprises people, and Column Week's fifth incident is it happening: a workload moved from local NVMe onto a fabric, same compute, same data. Most queries improved. One class — small, highly selective, point-lookup-shaped reads issued at high concurrency by an application — degraded sharply, with

```
fabric throughput used     21%, flat for the whole window
requests / s                400  →  9,800
bytes per request           512 KB  →  9 KB      (a factor of 57 smaller)
p95 query latency            40 ms  →  610 ms
```

**21% utilisation while completely saturated.** If bandwidth were the constraint that figure would be near 100. Low utilisation with rising request count, falling request size and rising latency is a request-bound workload, and provisioning more bandwidth changes nothing at all — which is why the wrong answers on that incident card (oversubscribed fabric, "disaggregation is just slower", decode moved to the compute nodes) each cost real money in a real meeting. The fix is coalescing reads, raising the minimum read granularity, prefetching by column chunk and raising concurrency, then re-measuring the two numbers rather than the product.

## The two ceilings on elasticity that are yours, not the platform's

Elasticity is the ability to add workers. It is worth exactly as much as your work is divisible, and two things in this course already bounded that.

**Ceiling 1 — the granularity of parallelism is set by your physical layout.** If the unit a scan can hand out is a row group, a table with fewer row groups than you have cores cannot use your cores (C4.L5). So the cluster you *can* provision and the cluster you can *use* are different numbers, and the second one was fixed months earlier by whoever sized the row groups on the loader. An elastic platform does not repair a table with eight row groups; it just lets you pay for 64 workers to watch eight of them work.

**Ceiling 2 — a hot key cannot be divided at all.** C6.L3 measured this rather than argued it: taking the same job from 32 partitions to 128 moved the mean from 15,000 rows to 3,750 and left the busiest partition at **221,591 of its original 227,801 rows**, so the skew ratio went from 15.19 to **59.09** and the job was no faster. Elasticity assumes work divides. A single key is the counter-example, and it is common.

Put those together and you get the sentence to say when somebody proposes solving a slow job by scaling the cluster: **"Adding workers multiplies throughput only over work that can be split. Show me the row-group count and the max-over-mean partition ratio, and I will tell you what the next 32 workers will do."** Both numbers are available from metadata, before anyone provisions anything.

**And the honest caveat about the 12×.** The elastic arithmetic above assumes the workload really is bursty, that spin-up is short relative to the burst, and that the platform's billing unit can actually go to zero — three assumptions, none of which is a property of the architecture. State them yourself: a disaggregated platform running a cluster 24 hours a day has bought the round trips and declined the elasticity, which is the worst cell in the table.

_fig 1 — what disaggregation buys, what it charges, and the two ceilings that are yours_

Components: one copy of the table on shared storage (no compute node owns any of it) · independent scaling (storage grows without buying CPUs) · disposable compute (64 worker-hours instead of 768) · one copy, many engines (pipelines you did not build) · and the storage layer moved further away, permanently (round trips are now a first-class resource (C0.L3 — not re-derived here)) · 21% throughput used, and saturated (9,800 requests/s at 9 KB each · p95 40 → 610 ms) · elasticity you cannot use (more workers than row groups does nothing (C4.L5)) · and 4× the workers took the skew ratio from 15.19 to 59.09 (measured: the busiest partition kept 221,591 of 227,801 rows) · so quote two numbers, never the product (requests/s and bytes/request — plus row groups and max ÷ mean)

Steps:
1. Start from the property everything else follows from: one copy of the table on shared storage, owned by no compute node. C6.L4 showed what that does to node loss and rescale; this is what it does to a capacity plan.
2. Three purchases. Storage and compute scale on separate axes; a cluster needed two hours a day costs 64 worker-hours instead of 768, a factor of 12; and one open copy removes the pipelines that keep private copies in sync.
3. All three are charged for in the same currency: the storage layer is further away and stays there. Round trips become a resource you have to manage, and row group size stops being only a pruning decision and becomes your request size.
4. Column Week's fifth incident is the bill arriving. Fabric throughput sits at 21% for the whole window while request rate climbs to 9,800 per second at 9 KB each and p95 latency goes from 40 ms to 610 ms — saturated on requests, idle on bytes.
5. Then two ceilings that belong to you rather than to the platform. The first is granularity: if a scan hands out row groups, a table with fewer row groups than cores cannot use the cores you elastically provisioned.
6. The second is indivisible work. Measured in the skew lab: quadrupling the partition count took the ratio from 15.19 to 59.09 because the mean fell and the hot key did not move. Elasticity multiplies only what splits.
7. So the reporting discipline is the same one C0.L3 established, plus two layout numbers: requests per second and bytes per request for the storage regime, row-group count and max over mean for how much of the cluster your work can actually reach.

- **12×** — worker-hours saved by a genuinely two-hour workload (32 workers for 2 hours is 64 worker-hours against 768 always-on. A count, and it holds only if the workload is really bursty and the billing unit can reach zero.)
- **21%** — fabric throughput used while completely saturated (Column Week's fifth incident, flat for the whole window. If bandwidth were the constraint this would be near 100 — the constraint was round trips.)
- **57×** — drop in bytes per request during that incident (512 KB per request became 9 KB while request rate went from 400/s to 9,800/s. Same bytes, vastly more round trips, and p95 latency from 40 ms to 610 ms.)

> **[info]** A1 and A2 will ask you to write this properly. The skeleton is four claims, each with a count and a named assumption:
> 
> 1. **What we stop buying.** "Storage grows 40% a year and query compute does not, so those become separate lines." The number is the growth rate and the mechanism behind it (C0.L5's growth term).
> 2. **What elasticity is worth here.** "Our reporting shape is two hours a day at 32 workers: 64 worker-hours against 768." The assumption is that the shape is real — bring the query-arrival histogram, not an intuition.
> 3. **What it costs us in round trips.** "Our worst class is small selective reads at high concurrency; we will measure requests per second and bytes per request, because that class regressed on the last migration at 21% throughput utilisation." The assumption is that our current request sizes survive the move, which is a layout question (row group and page size).
> 4. **What we cannot use.** "Tables with fewer row groups than workers and any exchange with a max-over-mean above about 3 will not benefit from added compute." The assumption is that the layout stays as designed, which is precisely what Column Week's first incident says does not happen on its own.
> 
> Four claims, four counts, four assumptions stated before the room finds them. That is the difference between a memo and a pitch.

---

> **[warning]** The failure mode of this architecture is not a technical one, it is an organisational one: a platform is migrated onto disaggregated storage, and then the cluster runs 24 hours a day because a team needs interactive latency at 09:00 and nobody wants to explain a cold start.
> 
> You have then paid the full price — every scan is a remote read, every small-read workload is one refactor away from Column Week's fifth incident, every cache is cold after a deploy — and collected none of the 12×. It is the worst cell in the table, and it is common, because the costs are structural and immediate while the benefit requires somebody to actually turn compute off.
> 
> If that is your situation, the number to bring is not throughput. It is **hours per day at each cluster size**, next to **queries per hour by class**. Those two together tell you whether the shape supports elasticity at all — and if it does not, the architecture choice deserves revisiting on its other merits (one copy, many engines; node loss without a rebuild) rather than on an elasticity argument you are not collecting.

_disaggregation ≡ three moves you have already lived through_

- **autoscaling a stateless web tier** (Trivial once no instance holds session state, and the reason it was hard before was exactly that they did. Scaling is easy when nothing is owned.) ≡ **resizing a compute cluster between queries** (Same precondition, same payoff: no node owns a slice, so the resize is a process count rather than a data migration — and the state that remains is a cache, so losing it is a first-query cost.)
- **cattle, not pets** (A machine you can destroy without ceremony is a machine you can replace during an incident. The discipline that makes it possible is refusing to put unique state on it.) ≡ **disposable compute** (A worker dying mid-query becomes a retry rather than a rebuild — but only because no data was local. The same property is what makes spot capacity usable for analytics.)
- **a shared filesystem instead of per-team exports** (One copy that everyone reads removes a class of bug — the stale export, the divergent transform, the pipeline nobody owns — and it makes access control the new hard problem.) ≡ **one open copy, many engines** (Identical trade at table scale: no pipelines between engines, and now the catalog is the control plane and schema evolution is a contract with consumers (A2).)

**Q1. A migration to disaggregated storage is proposed with a business case built entirely on scaling compute down outside business hours. What is the single most important thing to check before agreeing, and why?**
   A. The published throughput of the storage layer, since every query now reads remotely and bandwidth becomes the shared constraint
   B. The query-arrival shape by hour and class, because the entire case is "the cluster can be off" — and a platform that ends up running 24 hours a day has bought every cost of the distance and collected none of the 12× in worker-hours
   C. The compression ratio achievable in the new format, since storage volume drives the ongoing commitment
   D. Whether the engine supports the same SQL dialect, since a rewrite would dominate the effort
   Answer: B — The business case is a claim about a shape, so the shape is what to verify: 32 workers for 2 hours is 64 worker-hours against 768, and that factor of 12 exists only if the compute genuinely goes away. The costs, by contrast, are structural and arrive on day one — every scan is remote, small-read workloads become request-bound, and caches are cold after every resize. Throughput is the wrong number for the reason C6.L4 gave, and it is not where these migrations fail. Compression and dialect compatibility are real project risks and neither one is the assumption the case rests on. Naming the load-bearing assumption before the room does is the habit this course grades.

**Q2. After a migration, one application-facing query class has p95 latency of 610 ms against 40 ms before, request rate has gone from 400/s to 9,800/s, bytes per request from 512 KB to 9 KB, and fabric throughput utilisation is 21%. A vendor offers additional bandwidth. What do you do?**
   A. Take the bandwidth: the fabric is clearly the new bottleneck since latency rose after the storage layer changed
   B. Decline it and fix request size: 21% utilisation with a 57× drop in bytes per request means the workload is request-bound, so coalesce adjacent reads, raise the minimum read granularity, prefetch by column chunk and raise concurrency — then re-measure requests per second and bytes per request rather than the product
   C. Move this query class back to local NVMe and keep everything else disaggregated
   D. Add compute nodes so more requests can be issued in parallel
   Answer: B — The 21% figure is the diagnosis. Bandwidth is the product of request rate and request size, so a saturated bandwidth-bound workload sits near 100% — low utilisation with a rising request count and a collapsing request size is the signature of a round-trip-bound workload, and more bandwidth is capacity you are already not using. The 9 KB request size is the actual defect and it usually traces to layout: row groups or pages sized for a local disk, plus a reader that is not coalescing adjacent ranges. Splitting the workload back onto local storage solves it by abandoning the architecture and introduces a coherence problem. Adding compute raises the request rate, which is the term already saturated — it makes the graph worse and the bill larger.

**Q3. A team asks for the cluster to be doubled to fix a nightly job that has slowed down. Which two metadata numbers do you ask for first, and what will they tell you?**
   A. Total bytes scanned and compression ratio — together they say how much data the job is really reading
   B. Row-group count for the largest table and max ÷ mean partition size for the job's exchange — the first bounds how many workers the scan can even hand work to, and the second says whether the work divides at all, since quadrupling workers on a hot key took a measured skew ratio from 15.19 to 59.09
   C. Cluster CPU utilisation and memory headroom — they show whether the current cluster is actually the constraint
   D. Query count per hour and average query duration — they show whether the slowdown is this job or contention
   Answer: B — Both requested numbers come from metadata, cost nothing to obtain and can each independently invalidate the request. If the scan hands out row groups and the table has fewer row groups than the current worker count, the extra workers have nothing to be given (C4.L5). If the exchange has one heavy key, added workers lower the mean and leave the busiest partition intact — measured in the skew lab as 15.19 becoming 59.09 at 4× the parallelism, with the busiest partition retaining 221,591 of its 227,801 rows. Bytes scanned and compression describe the read side and say nothing about divisibility. Utilisation is ambiguous: a skewed job shows low utilisation and so does an idle one. Contention metrics are worth having second, after you know whether the proposed fix could work at all.

**Vuppalapati, Miron, Agarwal, Truong, Motivala and Cruanes, "Building an Elastic Query Engine on Disaggregated Storage" (NSDI 2020)** is the most useful paper on this page: it reports production telemetry from a large disaggregated warehouse and quantifies the things this lesson asserts — how much of the working set the ephemeral cache actually captures, how skewed real query resource demands are, and how often the elasticity is used at all. Read it before writing any elasticity memo.

**"The Snowflake Elastic Data Warehouse" (SIGMOD 2016)** is the design statement it follows up on, and the section to read twice is the one on the local ephemeral cache, because that is the design's answer to the cost this lesson names.

**Durner, Leis and Neumann, "Exploiting Cloud Object Storage for High-Performance Analytics" (VLDB 2023)** is the practitioner's version of the request-size argument: it measures how request size, concurrency and instance network limits interact, and it is the strongest available evidence that "the network is the new disk" needs the qualification C0.L3 gives it.

**Hellerstein et al., "Serverless Computing: One Step Forward, Two Steps Back" (CIDR 2019)** is the useful counterweight. Its central complaint — that disaggregated, stateless compute makes data movement the dominant cost and removes the programmer's ability to control locality — is exactly the tension between this lesson's two halves, argued by people who wanted the elastic version to work.

For the read-path mechanics, the **Arrow and Parquet range-coalescing implementations** and the **DuckDB documentation on reading remote files** are where the fixes in the second quiz answer actually live; reading either one's read-planning code teaches more than any blog post about latency.

**Where C6 lands.** You can now cost an exchange in bytes, choose between the two join strategies from arithmetic, recognise the one partition that owns the wall clock, compare three architectures on the two events that matter, and price elasticity in both directions. **C7** takes the data that does not fit the flat columnar model — nested structures, evolving schemas, Arrow as the boundary, and vectors living in a columnar table — and after that the architecture half asks you to defend all of it in a room.
